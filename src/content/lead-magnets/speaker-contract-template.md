# Speaker contract template

The whole agreement, clause by clause, with every blank in square brackets and a plain-English note on what each clause is protecting.

A speaker agreement does not have to be long to work. It has to be specific. Most of the ones that go wrong are one page long and say things like "the speaker will be involved in the conference" and "we will cover travel" — sentences that felt generous when they were written and turn into an argument two weeks before the event.

Some people call this a speaker agreement rather than a speaker contract. It is the same document, and the two words are used interchangeably here — if a speaker's office asks for an agreement template, this is the one to send.

What follows is the whole document. Eighteen clauses, in the order they belong, with every blank in square brackets. Beside each clause is a short note in plain English: what the clause is protecting you against, and what goes wrong when it is missing. Then an index of every blank so you can fill them in one pass, a section on what to change and what not to change, and what to do if the speaker sends you their own paper instead.

Copy it, fill in the brackets, delete what does not apply, and send it. There is no download, and it prints.

**This is a starting point, not legal advice.** It is written to be usable by a small event without a lawyer, and it is deliberately plain. If the fee is large, or this is the biggest event your organization runs, have a lawyer read it once. An hour of a lawyer's time on a document you have already drafted is far cheaper than the same hour starting from a blank page.

**The document is the last step, not the first.** Two other pages come before it. If the person has not been asked yet, the [speaker invitation letter template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-invitation-letter) has three complete letters and the four things to settle before one goes out. If the terms are still being negotiated — the fee, the travel cap, what happens if either side cancels, who owns the recording — the questions belong on the [venue and speaker contract checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/vendor-contract-checklist), which takes them one at a time and is where the answers come from.

**This page does not repeat those questions, on purpose.** A page that restated them would be two documents pretending to be one. The checklist is the negotiation. This page assumes those answers exist and needs somewhere to write them down. Read the checklist with the speaker's terms in front of you, then come back and put the answers into the brackets below.

## How to use this in ten minutes

1. **Copy the clauses** into a document — Word, Google Docs, anything. The text between the blockquotes is the agreement.
2. **Fill every bracket.** If a bracket does not apply, delete it rather than leaving it blank; a document with `[DATE]` still in it tells the other side you were not paying attention.
3. **Delete the clauses that genuinely do not apply.** A speaker who is local and driving does not need clause 5 in full.
4. **Keep the two-sided cancellation and the recording clause** even if the rest of the document is short. Those are the two that are free to write now and expensive to fix later.
5. **Send one version.** Save it as a PDF so the other side is not editing a live copy, and ask them to reply with a yes and their name and date — or sign it and send it back.
6. **Keep the signed copy where the money lives**, not in an email thread. If you are running the event in Convocate, that is the contract record; if you are not, it is the same folder as the budget.

## The agreement

*Everything from here to the signature lines is the document. The notes under each clause are for you, not for the speaker — delete them before you send.*

> **SPEAKER AGREEMENT**
>
> This agreement is between [ORGANIZER LEGAL NAME], of [ORGANIZER ADDRESS] ("the organizer"), and [SPEAKER FULL NAME], of [SPEAKER ADDRESS] ("the speaker"), for the event described below. It takes effect on the date of the last signature.

### 1. The event

> The event is [EVENT NAME], taking place on [EVENT DATES] at [VENUE NAME], [VENUE ADDRESS], [CITY, STATE].

**What this protects.** It names the thing being agreed. Most of these disputes are not about money — they are about which event, which city, which date. A speaker with a full calendar can honestly believe they agreed to the gathering in one city and arrive in another. The venue address is not decoration either: it is what a travel budget gets priced against, and what decides whose airport it is.

### 2. The engagement

> The speaker will present [SESSION TITLE], a [LENGTH]-minute [keynote / workshop / breakout session / panel], on [DAY, DATE] from [START TIME] to [END TIME] in [ROOM], including [NUMBER] minutes for questions. This is [one] session. A second session, a panel appearance or a book signing is not covered by this agreement unless it is written here: [ADDITIONS, OR "none"].

**What this protects.** This is the clause every later argument refers back to. "Be involved in the conference" has no edge to it, and an edge is what you need when you are building a program in April and the session was agreed in November. Minutes, a start time, an end time and a count of sessions settle, in one sentence, whether a second talk is a favor or a second fee.

### 3. The fee

> The organizer will pay the speaker a fee of [$FEE AMOUNT] ([FEE AMOUNT IN WORDS]) for the engagement in clause 2. The fee is [inclusive / exclusive] of any taxes. It covers preparation, the session, and [slides or handouts]. It does not cover travel, lodging, ground transport or meals, which are handled under clause 5.

**What this protects.** Three things get argued about a fee: the number, whether tax is inside it, and what it buys. The number is yours to choose, and it belongs on its own line in the [conference budget template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-budget-template) with travel beside it rather than inside it. Whether tax sits inside or outside is a question about your own tax position — put it to your treasurer or accountant and write down the answer rather than guessing on a contract. And the scope sentence is what stops "the fee" from quietly meaning one 45-minute talk when the speaker thought it meant the talk and the panel.

If your organization calls this an honorarium rather than a fee, use that word throughout — but keep the same three parts: the amount, the tax wording, and what it covers.

### 4. Payment

> The organizer will pay [$DEPOSIT] on [DEPOSIT DUE DATE] as a deposit, and the balance of [$BALANCE] [on or before EVENT DATE / within NUMBER days of the event, on invoice]. Payment is by [check / bank transfer / card] to [PAYEE NAME AND PAYMENT DETAILS]. The speaker will send an invoice to [ORGANIZER CONTACT NAME, EMAIL] by [INVOICE DATE]. Any bank or card fee is paid by [the organizer / the speaker].

**What this protects.** A deposit with a date on it is what turns a conversation into a booking, for both of you. The date matters more than the amount: "we will send a deposit" is not a commitment anyone can check. Naming the person who approves payment matters too, because a committee inbox loses things and a speaker who has not been paid by the morning of the event is a problem in the room, not in the accounts.

### 5. Travel and lodging

> The organizer will [book and pay for / reimburse] travel to and from [CITY] by [MODE AND CLASS], up to a maximum of [$TRAVEL CAP]. The organizer will provide [NUMBER] nights of lodging at [HOTEL NAME OR "a hotel of the organizer's choosing"] for the nights of [LODGING DATES]. [Ground transport, parking and meals up to $AMOUNT per day are / are not] reimbursed. Reimbursement is against receipts, sent to [NAME] by [RECEIPT DEADLINE]. Whoever books the travel is responsible for change fees.

**What this protects.** "We will cover your travel" is not a number, and it is the line that breaks a small budget, because a flight booked three weeks out costs what it costs. A cap turns an open promise into a known figure you can put on the budget. Naming who books decides who pays the change fee when a connection moves. And the nights matter: a speaker on at nine in the morning who flies in that morning is a coin flip you have chosen to take.

### 6. What the organizer provides

> The organizer will provide, at no cost to the speaker: [MICROPHONE TYPE], [projector and screen / confidence monitor / neither], [a tuned piano / no instrument], [a lectern], wireless internet, a quiet space from [TIME], parking at [LOCATION], and an on-site contact — [NAME], [PHONE] — who will meet the speaker at [TIME AND PLACE] on arrival.

**What this protects.** Most speaker problems on the day are technical and boring, and almost all of them are avoidable in one sentence. Written down, this becomes the AV team's job list. Left unwritten, it becomes a conversation in a corridor ten minutes before the session, with no one who can fix it.

### 7. Program information and materials

> The speaker will send [SESSION TITLE, a 50-word description, a 100-word bio, a headshot, and any slides] to [NAME, EMAIL] by [MATERIALS DEADLINE]. The organizer may use these in the printed program, the event website, the event app and event promotion, before and after the event. The speaker keeps copyright in their slides and materials.

**What this protects.** The deadline is the point of the clause. Programs go to the printer on a date whether or not a bio has arrived, and the person chasing it is usually the person with the least time. Saying plainly that the speaker keeps copyright in their slides is also what makes the permission in the next sentence easy to give — you are licensing the program use, not taking the work.

### 8. Recording, photography and use of name and likeness

> The organizer may [video and audio record / audio record only / not record] the session. The organizer [may / may not] publish the recording publicly, [may / may not] use short clips in promotion, and [may / may not] sell or license the recording. The speaker [may / may not] record and publish their own session. The organizer may use the speaker's name, photograph and session title in promotion of this event; that permission ends [on the last day of the event / NUMBER months after the event], except for pages already published. Ownership of the recording is [the organizer's / shared with the speaker].

**What this protects.** This is the cheapest clause to settle before the event and the most expensive to settle after it, because after the event the recording exists and the leverage is gone. If nothing is written, you probably cannot post the session at all, and the speaker may be able to use footage of your event however they like. The end date on the name-and-photo permission is doing quiet work too: "forever, everywhere" is not a reasonable ask, and a careful speaker will hesitate over a document that demands it.

### 9. Books, products and promotion from the stage

> The speaker [may / may not] sell books or products at the event, at [LOCATION], with [all proceeds to the speaker / NUMBER% of proceeds to the organizer]. The speaker [may / may not] mention their own programs, courses or services during the session, for no more than [NUMBER] minutes. The organizer [will / will not] offer a book table or pre-order information to attendees.

**What this protects.** An unannounced sales pitch in the middle of a keynote is the thing organizers most resent and can least do anything about at the time. A number of minutes and a place for the table is a complete answer, and it is much easier to agree to now than to interrupt later.

### 10. Promotion

> Each party may name the other and the event in its own promotion. Neither party will describe the other's involvement in a way the other has not agreed. The organizer will not use the speaker's name to suggest an endorsement of any product or organization other than this event.

**What this protects.** It keeps a good sentence from becoming a claim nobody approved — a quote on a website that reads as an endorsement of something the speaker has never heard of. It cuts both ways, which is why speakers accept it.

### 11. If the speaker cancels

> If the speaker cannot take part, they will tell the organizer in writing as soon as they know. If the cancellation is for a reason other than [illness, a family emergency, a travel disruption outside the speaker's control, or another event beyond the speaker's control], the deposit is returned within [NUMBER] business days and the organizer owes nothing further. If the cancellation is for one of those reasons, [the deposit is returned / the deposit is held against a future event within 12 months]. The speaker will [help the organizer find a replacement / record the session / neither].

**What this protects.** There are two halves here and they are both load-bearing. The first half is yours: without it, a speaker can cancel and keep the deposit, and you are starting the search again with less money than you budgeted. The second half is what makes the first half fair enough to sign — a speaker with a documented reason is not the risk you are pricing. Naming what counts as a reason stops the later argument about whether a reason was good enough. And the last sentence costs nothing to write; when it is honored, it is worth more than the deposit.

### 12. If the organizer cancels or postpones

> If the organizer cancels the event, it will tell the speaker in writing and will pay: [the deposit only, if the cancellation is more than NUMBER days before the event / the deposit and NUMBER% of the fee if it is between NUMBER and NUMBER days before / the full fee if it is within NUMBER days]. If the organizer postpones to a date within [NUMBER] months, the fee and the deposit carry to the new date and this agreement applies to it. If the organizer cancels for a reason beyond its control — weather, a government order, the venue becoming unavailable — it will pay [the deposit / reasonable unrecoverable travel costs up to $AMOUNT].

**What this protects.** This is the mirror of clause 11, and it is the clause that decides whether a careful speaker signs at all. A document whose cancellation terms only protect the organizer reads as one-sided, and the experienced speaker will either refuse it or price the risk into the fee. The postponement sentence is the practical one: without it, moving your own event by three months means renegotiating the fee from a weaker position.

Set the percentages to what you could actually pay if the event collapsed. A clause you cannot honor is worse than no clause, because it is the one that gets quoted back to you.

### 13. Changes to the date, time or program

> Neither party may change the date, the time or the length of the session without the other's written agreement. If the organizer moves the session to another day within the same event, the speaker may decline, and this agreement then ends under clause 12.

**What this protects.** A session moved to a 7 a.m. slot, or a date moved by three weeks, is a different booking for the speaker and often a different trip. Written agreement means neither side finds out about the change on arrival.

### 14. Independent contractor

> The speaker is an independent contractor, not an employee or agent of the organizer, and is responsible for their own taxes, insurance and benefits. The speaker will provide [the tax form the organizer requires] before payment.

**What this protects.** It says plainly what everyone in the room assumes and nobody writes down: this is a booking, not a job. If the amount is large, ask your accountant which form you need — that is a question about your own tax position, and this template cannot answer it for you.

### 15. Conduct and the venue's rules

> The speaker will follow the venue's reasonable rules and the organizer's code of conduct for the event, a copy of which is [attached / at this web address]. If the speaker's conduct makes the event unsafe or puts the venue's license at risk, the organizer may end the engagement, and will still pay the fee for any session already delivered.

**What this protects.** A speaker agreement is not the right document to solve conduct, but one sentence pointing at a code you already have costs nothing and gives you a place to stand. Keep it narrow: it should be about safety and the venue, not about content.

### 16. Notices

> Notices under this agreement go to [ORGANIZER NAME, EMAIL, PHONE] and [SPEAKER NAME, EMAIL, PHONE]. Email is enough.

**What this protects.** Several clauses above say "in writing". This one says where. Without it, "in writing" means "whenever someone remembers to", which is the same as not having it.

### 17. The whole agreement

> This agreement is the whole agreement between the parties on this subject and replaces anything discussed before it. It can only be changed in writing, agreed by both parties by signature or by email.

**What this protects.** This is what stops a hallway promise from becoming a term months later — and it protects the speaker from the same thing in the other direction. The "or by email" half is deliberate: most small changes happen by email, and a clause that only recognizes signatures will be ignored by both sides and then argued about.

### 18. Governing law

> This agreement is governed by the law of [STATE, PROVINCE OR COUNTRY], and any dispute will be heard in [COUNTY OR CITY].

**What this protects.** This is the one line left blank on purpose, and it is the one line worth a lawyer's eye if the fee is large. Which law applies is a question about where you are and what your organization is, and a template written for anyone cannot answer it for you.

### Signatures

> Signed for the organizer: [NAME], [TITLE], for [ORGANIZATION] — Date: [DATE]
>
> Signed by the speaker: [NAME] — Date: [DATE]

A typed name and a date is what most small events use, and it holds up as well as the document under it is specific — which is the whole argument for writing clauses instead of sentences. It is not a qualified electronic signature, and this page will not pretend otherwise. If the amount is large, or your organization requires one, use a signature service, or print it, sign it, scan it and email the scan.

## What to change, and what not to change

**Change these freely. They are yours, and a template cannot guess them.**

- **The fee, the deposit split and the payment dates.** Every number in clauses 3 and 4 is a decision about your budget, not about the law.
- **The travel cap, the class and the nights.** Set the cap to what your budget can absorb, and the nights to what the schedule needs.
- **The cancellation bands in clause 12.** Set them to what you could actually pay if the event collapsed.
- **How long the name-and-photo permission lasts.** Twelve months, or until the next event, is easier to agree to than forever.
- **The minutes allowed for promotion from the stage.** Zero is a legitimate answer.
- **Whether you record at all, and whether you may post it.** Many small events are better off saying no to recording and keeping the conversation simple.
- **The governing law line.** Change it — but with advice if the amount is large.
- **Add clauses for what you actually have.** Accessibility requirements, a second session, a panel, a virtual appearance, a signed-book arrangement. Adding is normal; this document is a floor, not a ceiling.

**Do not change these without thinking about why they are written that way.**

- **The legal names of both parties.** A nickname and an abbreviation on a contract is a contract you will be explaining later, usually to somebody's accountant.
- **The engagement description in clause 2.** Every other clause refers back to it. Shortening it to "keynote" after you agreed 45 minutes with questions quietly deletes the questions.
- **Either half of the cancellation.** If you delete clause 12 to keep the document friendly, you have a one-sided agreement, and the experienced speaker will notice and price it in.
- **"In writing" in clauses 13 and 17.** That phrase is what makes an email a record. Removing it means every change is a memory.
- **The independent contractor line.** Do not delete it to make the document feel warmer. If you are unsure what it should say for your organization, ask your accountant, not the speaker.
- **A blank.** A bracket left in a signed document is a hole someone else will fill in later. Every blank is filled or deleted.

**And one rule over all of it: do not add a clause you cannot explain out loud.** If somebody sends you a line about liquidated damages, indemnification or a hold-harmless and you cannot say in one sentence what it does, either delete it or get it explained. A clause nobody understands is not protection; it is a surprise with a date on it.

## When the speaker sends their own paper instead

Sometimes the reply is their agreement, not yours. That is normal, especially with someone who speaks often, and it is not a sign of trouble.

Read it against this one clause by clause, and remember the two documents are answering the same eighteen questions. Where theirs is silent, this page tells you what silence costs. Where theirs says something you cannot explain, ask.

Then **agree on one version.** The failure in a counter-draft is never the drafting; it is two Word files with different dates, one of which has a clause the other does not, and two people who each believe they signed the same thing. Put the agreed terms in one document, send it as a PDF, and get both names and dates on that copy. If you want to keep your own paper, the [venue and speaker contract checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) covers what a speaker's terms usually leave out, and it is written for exactly this moment.

## If you need it signed tonight

Six clauses, and the rest can follow by email before the event. These six cannot, because they are the ones that get argued about.

1. **Who** — both legal names (clause 1).
2. **What and when** — the session, the date, the start and end time (clause 2).
3. **The fee** — the amount, and whether tax is inside it (clause 3).
4. **When it is paid** — deposit date and balance date (clause 4).
5. **Cancellation, both directions** — clauses 11 and 12.
6. **Recording and use of name** — clause 8.

That is a page and a half. It is a real agreement, and it is enormously better than an email that says "looking forward to it."
