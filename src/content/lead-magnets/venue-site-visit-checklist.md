# Venue site visit checklist

What to bring, what to measure, what to look at that the venue's own photos never show, and how to write it up so the negotiation starts from facts.

The site visit is the cheapest hour in the whole project, and it is usually the one people prepare for least. You will be met by somebody friendly, walked through the best room in the building, and handed a brochure with a photograph taken by a professional on the best day of the year. Two days later you will be asked to decide, and all you will remember is that it felt nice.

The room is a physical object. It has a length, a width, a ceiling height at its lowest beam, a number of outlets, a floor that makes a certain amount of noise, and a route from the street that a case has to travel. Every one of those facts turns into either a cost, a change to your plan, or a problem on the morning. You can get almost all of them in one visit with a tape measure and a pen.

This page is what to do in that hour. Every item is something you can tick, count, or write a number beside. It ends with a notes sheet you can print and fill in as you walk.

**What this page does not do.** It does not repeat the contract questions. The [venue and speaker contract checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) owns what has to be in writing before you sign — the deposit ladder, who pays for what, insurance, cancellation, the clauses people regret. This page is the tour: what you measure, what you photograph, what you ask while you are standing in the room, and how you record it. Visit first, then the checklist. If you read them the other way around, you will be negotiating about a room you have not measured.

**How to use it.** Print it, or keep it open on your phone. Tick as you go, and fill in the notes sheet at the end before you leave the parking lot. The notes sheet is the part you cannot skip — the numbers you write down are what you compare venues on, not the brochure and not the impression. If you would rather carry one page than a stack, print the notes sheet and read the sections above it on the way.

## Before you go

### The call, two days before

Most of the wasted time on a site visit is caused by things that could have been settled on the phone. Ask these when you book the appointment.

- [ ] **Which room will I actually be shown, and is it the room held for my date?** If the answer is "we will show you the ballroom, but your date is in the junior ballroom," ask to see the one you would be booking, even if it is set up for something else. A room you did not see is a room you are guessing about.
- [ ] **Can you send the floor plan with the dimensions on it?** A PDF with numbers, not the brochure drawing with no scale. If the only drawing they have is decorative, that tells you how they think about the room.
- [ ] **What set-up will the room be in when I arrive, and can it be changed while I am there?** An empty room and a banquet set are different rooms with different sightlines and different capacity. Seeing only one of them is seeing half the venue.
- [ ] **Can I come at the same time of day my event starts?** Light, street traffic, kitchen noise and parking availability are all different at 9 a.m. and at 6 p.m. If your event runs into the evening, try to see the room in the evening.
- [ ] **Who am I meeting, and what can that person agree to?** If the person showing you around cannot quote a rate or hold a date, ask who can and whether they can be there for ten minutes. A tour with the person who has the numbers is worth two tours without.
- [ ] **Can you hold the date in writing before I visit, and when does the hold expire?** A hold that nobody wrote down is not a hold. The expiry date goes in your notes.
- [ ] **Can I see the loading area, the kitchen and the AV position on the same visit?** Some venues will show you the room and nothing else. Ask before you arrive, because the back of house is where the costs hide.

### The bag

- [ ] **A tape measure, 25 feet or longer.** A phone measuring app is fine for a chair and poor for a room. If you only have the app, use it on the short distances and pace the long ones.
- [ ] **The floor plan you were sent, printed.** You will want to draw on it.
- [ ] **This page, printed, and a pen.** Phone notes work until you are in a basement with no signal.
- [ ] **A charged phone, for photographs.** See the photograph list below.
- [ ] **A small flashlight, or your phone's light.** For behind a riser, under a stage skirt, inside a dim control room, and up at the ceiling.
- [ ] **Your attendance estimate, and the number you expect at the largest single session.** Every measurement on this page is checked against that number. Without it, "big enough" means nothing.
- [ ] **A one-page description of your event:** the dates, the hours, the shape of a day, whether food is served, whether there is music, and what the largest session is. Handing this to the person showing you around changes the tour, because they stop showing you a generic room and start answering your questions.

## On arrival

Do this before the tour starts, while you are still an ordinary visitor. It takes fifteen minutes and it is the part the venue cannot stage.

- [ ] **Park where an attendee will park.** Then time the walk from the car to the front door, at a normal pace, carrying something. That number is the first thing attendees will mention and the last thing anybody plans for.
- [ ] **Walk in through the front door, not the staff entrance.** Where is the sign that tells a stranger where to go? If you cannot find it in daylight with an appointment, neither will two hundred people at once.
- [ ] **Time the walk from the front door to the room.** Then note what is on the way: stairs, a long corridor, a flight of steps with no ramp, a door that needs a key.
- [ ] **Count the restrooms near the room and note whether they are on the same floor.** Divide by your largest session. If the line at the break will be longer than the break, you have found a real problem, and it is one the venue will not raise.
- [ ] **Look at the space where check-in would go.** Pace out the run of table you would need, then see what it blocks: a door, a corridor, a fire exit, the only route to the restrooms. Check-in that blocks an exit is not an option, however convenient it looks.
- [ ] **Test the wifi on your own phone, in the room, while the person is standing next to you.** Ask for the guest network name and whether it is shared with the rest of the building. A speed test on a Tuesday afternoon with four people in the building tells you little, but a network that fails that test tells you everything.
- [ ] **Check your phone signal in the room.** Your team, the speaker and the AV crew will all be on phones all day. A room with no signal is a room where your staff cannot talk to each other.
- [ ] **Ask whether the room has its own thermostat and who controls it.** A room with no local control at seven in the morning will be whatever temperature the building decided overnight.
- [ ] **Stand still and listen with the room empty and the air off.** Write down what you hear: a fan, an extractor, a road, an elevator, the room next door. Then ask for the air to be turned on and listen again. Rooms have a sound even when they are quiet, and it is the sound a speaker has to talk over.
- [ ] **Ask what else is booked in the building on your date, and at what times.** A wedding downstairs, a basketball league in the parking lot and a conference in the next room are all facts about your noise and your parking, and none of them appear in the brochure.

## The room: measure it, count it, sit in it

### Measure

- [ ] **The clear inside length and width, wall to wall, at floor level.** Two numbers, written down. Not the dimensions on the sheet — the ones your tape says.
- [ ] **The ceiling height at its highest point and at its lowest.** Under a beam, a duct, a light fixture or a projector bracket is where a screen, a truss or a tall backdrop stops fitting. The lowest point is the number that matters.
- [ ] **The distance from the back wall to where the stage would go.** That distance decides whether the back row sees a face, or only a screen.
- [ ] **The width of the widest door into the room, and the narrowest point on the route from the loading area to the room.** The narrowest point decides what you can bring in. Measure it rather than looking at it, because a corridor that looks fine is often two inches short of a road case.
- [ ] **Where the stage goes, and whether it can be moved.** Note how much floor the stage takes when it is in place, and whether that is deducted from the capacity you were quoted.

### Count

- [ ] **Count the chairs, if the room is set.** Do not take the number on the sheet. Count.
- [ ] **Count the tables, and note their size and shape.** Round tables of ten, six-foot rectangles and cocktail tables all produce different rooms from the same floor.
- [ ] **Count the power outlets you can see, and mark where they are.** Then ask how many are on the same circuit. You cannot tell by looking, and the answer decides whether the AV desk and the coffee urn can run at the same time.
- [ ] **Count the exits from the room, and note where they are relative to the stage.** A room where everyone leaves through the same door that everyone arrived through is a room with a bottleneck.
- [ ] **Count the columns, low ceilings and hanging fixtures, and mark where they fall.** A column in the middle of a room costs seats that the capacity number still counts, and it can cost a sightline in the middle of the room.

### Sightlines

- [ ] **Sit in the worst seat you can find** — front row at the far side, behind a column, under the balcony, next to the door — and look at the stage. If you cannot see, neither can the person who paid to be there.
- [ ] **Stand at the back corner and look at the front.** Note how much of the room is out of view from there, and how far away the speaker's face is.
- [ ] **Check where a screen would hang, and whether the front rows can see it without craning.** Screens that are right for the back row are often too high for the first three rows.
- [ ] **Check whether the floor is flat or raked, and whether the seats at the back can be raised.** On a flat floor with a low stage, the back half of the room is looking at the back of the person in front.

### The room itself

- [ ] **Look at the floor.** Carpet, tile, wood, and how much noise a chair makes when it is dragged. A tile floor in a room of 300 chairs is a sound all of its own.
- [ ] **Look at the windows and ask about blackout.** A room that faces the sun at four in the afternoon has to be able to be made dark, and blinds that "mostly close" are not blackout.
- [ ] **Test the sound with your voice, not the PA.** Stand where the speaker will stand and speak in a normal voice. Have the person with you stand at the back and tell you whether they can hear you. Then clap once and listen to how long the room rings.
- [ ] **Note what is fixed to the walls.** Screens, whiteboards, notice boards, artwork, a bar, a fireplace, a stage that does not move. Anything you cannot take down is something your plan has to work around.
- [ ] **Find the accessible route, and walk it.** Step-free from the parking area to the room, the accessible restroom on the same floor, and the places where a wheelchair sits without blocking an aisle. Ask which door is the accessible one, then use it.
- [ ] **Check where a speaker stands and whether they will be lit.** A dark stage with bright house lights is the most common thing nobody checks, and the photographs will show it if you take them.
- [ ] **Find the quiet room.** Where does a speaker wait before a session, and can they be alone? Ask whether it is included or a separate charge, and where it is relative to the room.

## Photographs: take these, in this order

The venue's photographs were taken at whatever hour flattered the room, by somebody who does this for a living. Yours are evidence.

1. **Each corner of the room from standing height**, with the lights on as they will be during a session.
2. **The room from the stage, and the stage from the back row.** Two photographs that answer the sightline question better than any drawing.
3. **The lowest point of the ceiling**, with something for scale in the frame — a sheet of paper is exactly 8.5 by 11 inches and works as a ruler in a photograph.
4. **Every door into the room**, including the width, with the tape measure held against the frame.
5. **The outlets and the electrical panel**, so you can find them again without a second visit.
6. **The route from the loading door to the room**, one photograph at each turn and each elevator.
7. **The thermostat and the clock on the wall**, if there is one. Both are things people argue about afterwards.
8. **The check-in area, and the wall behind it.** That wall is where your sign goes, and it is where the first line forms.
9. **The restrooms**, if you want that in the file. It is a strange thing to photograph and a useful thing to have.
10. **The floor, close up.** It decides what the chairs sound like and what a dropped cup looks like.

Put the date and the time of day in the filename, or in the first photograph of the set. Light at nine in the morning is not light at five in the afternoon, and the room you fell in love with in one of them may not be the room you get.

## The back of house

This is the half of the visit most people skip, and it is where a venue's real constraints live.

- [ ] **Walk the load-in route from the street to the room, and pace it.** Note every elevator, step, ramp, turn, threshold and stretch of carpet. A route that takes twenty minutes with a cart is a route that costs you an hour with a band.
- [ ] **Measure the loading door and the freight elevator:** width, height, and whether the elevator is shared with the rest of the building.
- [ ] **Ask when load-in starts and when load-out must be finished — as clock times.** "In the morning" is not a time, and the difference between an eight o'clock load-in and a ten o'clock one is a whole set-up.
- [ ] **Ask whether the room is yours for set-up the day before, and what that day costs.** If the answer is yes and the price is not on the sheet, you have found a line your budget is missing. It belongs on the [conference budget template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-budget-template) the same day you hear the number.
- [ ] **Find out where food comes in, if food is served:** which door, which elevator, where it waits before service, and how it gets to the room without crossing the session.
- [ ] **Ask who clears the room and when** — tables, chairs, trash — and what "leave it as you found it" means in practice.
- [ ] **Ask where empty cases and boxes go during the event, and who is responsible for them.** Every room you have ever seen at the end of a good event had a pile of something behind a curtain.
- [ ] **Find the AV control position.** In the room, at the back, in a closet, or in another room entirely? Whoever runs sound needs to see the stage, and a control position with no sightline is a problem you buy every day of the event.
- [ ] **Find the electrical panel, and ask whether you are allowed to touch it.**
- [ ] **Ask where deliveries go on the day, and who signs for them.** A pallet of programs that arrives at the wrong door is a morning you do not get back.
- [ ] **Ask whether there is a separate entrance for a speaker who would rather not cross the room**, and where it comes out.
- [ ] **Note where the trash goes, where the water is, and where staff eat and rest.** These sound trivial until hour ten of a long day.
- [ ] **Check the parking for your own team and for a speaker:** free, validated or charged, and where it is relative to the door they will use.

## The money questions

You are not negotiating in the room. You are collecting the numbers so that the negotiation, later and in writing, starts from something. Ask for the number, write it down, and say you will come back in writing. A venue that will not put a number in an email is telling you something worth knowing.

Each of these becomes a term in a contract later. Turning it into a clause is the job of the [venue and speaker contract checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) — this page stops at the number.

- [ ] **What time, exactly, does the room become yours on the day, and what time must you be out?** Both clock times, in writing.
- [ ] **What is the hourly overtime rate if the day runs long?** Ask whether it is charged per hour or per part hour, because the second one is the expensive one.
- [ ] **What does the set-up day cost, and is it cheaper if you take it with the event day?**
- [ ] **Does the rate change by day of the week or by season?** Ask for the rate for your date, not the rate on the sheet.
- [ ] **Is there a food and beverage minimum for your date, and what is the number?** If food is served, this is often the line that decides the whole budget, and it is usually not the first number they mention.
- [ ] **What percentage is the service charge, and is it charged on the room as well as on the food?**
- [ ] **May you bring your own coffee, water or wine, and what does the venue charge if you do?**
- [ ] **What does parking cost an attendee, per car, and who collects it?**
- [ ] **Is there a charge for wifi, for extra devices, or for a dedicated line?**
- [ ] **What does it cost to hang a banner or a sign, and where are you allowed to hang it?**
- [ ] **What does an extra hour of load-in cost?**
- [ ] **Who is the named on-site contact on the day, with a phone number, and will that person be in the building?** A venue where nobody on site can make a decision is a venue where your team improvises.
- [ ] **Ask what has been added to invoices at the end that the client did not expect, then ask for the list.** This is the question people are too polite to ask. The person showing you around usually answers it honestly, because it is not their money, and the answer is worth more than the brochure.
- [ ] **Ask for the quote in writing, dated and itemized, for your room and your dates, before you leave.** Itemized matters: one total tells you nothing about what is included.

Then, the same day, put the numbers into the [conference budget template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-budget-template) so the venue line is a figure rather than a range. A rate you heard and did not write down is a rate you will hear again, from memory, at a worse moment.

**What this page deliberately does not ask.** The deposit schedule, what is refundable and when, the cancellation ladder, insurance and certificates, and who is responsible for what on the final invoice. Those belong to the contract checklist, and they are the right questions to ask once you have decided you want the room. Asking them in the first ten minutes of a tour makes the rest of the tour worse, because the person showing you around stops talking.

## After the visit

The visit is worth very little if it is not written down within a day. Memory replaces numbers with feelings, and feelings cannot be compared across three venues.

- [ ] **Write the three things you would change about the room before you drive away.** Ten minutes in the parking lot is worth an hour next week, and it is the only time you will be able to see the room in your head.
- [ ] **Fill in the notes sheet at the end of this page the same day**, while the numbers are still numbers.
- [ ] **Rename the photographs** with the venue name and the date. A phone full of `IMG_4471` is not a record.
- [ ] **Send the venue a short email listing what you understood:** the dates, the room, the clock times, the numbers, and the two or three things you are still unsure about. Ask them to correct anything that is wrong. A venue that corrects you in writing has just given you the first draft of the contract. A venue that does not answer has told you something too.
- [ ] **Score it against the other venues on the same sheet, and only on the things that cost money or lose sleep.** Everything else is taste, and taste is not a reason to sign.
- [ ] **Write down the date the hold expires, and the next action with a date beside it.** Holds expire quietly.
- [ ] **Once you have a date and a room, the next thing out of the door is the speaker ask.** It is easier to write an invitation when you can name the room and the session, and the [speaker invitation letter template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-invitation-letter) has three complete letters and the four things to settle before one goes out.

## The five things people forget to check

Every visit has a blind spot. These are the five that show up later as a cost, an argument, or a change of plan.

**1. The room you are shown is not always the room you get.** Venues show the best room, on the best day, in the set-up that photographs well. The room held for your date may be smaller, on a different floor, or set for a dinner the night before. Ask to see the room that is actually held, in the set-up you will actually have, and write the room's name on the notes sheet. When the contract arrives, check that the name on it is the name you wrote down.

**2. The clock, not the room, is what you are renting.** You are buying a window: load-in from a certain time, out by a certain time, and a rate for every hour after that. Two venues with the same rental price can be hundreds of dollars apart once the window is counted, because one gives you the day before and the other gives you four hours. Ask for the window before you ask for the price.

**3. What the room sounds like from the other side of the wall.** You will listen for echo and for the air conditioning. What you will not hear, on a quiet Tuesday, is the room next door at full volume, the extractor fan that starts when the kitchen opens, the elevator bell, or the corridor that becomes a thoroughfare at lunch. Ask what is on the other side of each wall, and what time it starts.

**4. The two journeys: a person from a car to a seat, and a case from a van to the room.** They are different routes with different obstacles, both get measured by nobody, and both cost money. The first is what your attendees complain about. The second is what your AV crew charges you for. Walk both, and time both.

**5. The lowest point of the ceiling and the nearest outlet.** Not the height on the sheet, and not "there are plenty of outlets" — the beam, the duct, the projector bracket, and how many outlets share a circuit. These two facts decide whether the screen fits and whether the sound system can run, and they are the two that are hardest to fix after you have signed.
