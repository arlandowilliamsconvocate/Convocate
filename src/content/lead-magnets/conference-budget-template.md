# Conference Budget Template

A working budget for a 100 to 500 seat conference, built line by line: what each line pays for, what organizers most often forget to put in it, and a percentage guide you can check your own numbers against.

This is written for the person who owns the money for the event — the one who signs the contracts and answers when someone asks what the total is. You do not need software to use it. Copy it into a spreadsheet, or print the worksheet at the end.

---

## How to use this

1. **Start with your best guess at the total.** You need a number before you can check anything, and your first number will be wrong. That is expected.
2. **Fill in the lines you already know.** Venue and catering are usually the two biggest and usually the two you can price first.
3. **Check each line against the percentage guide below.** If one line is far outside its range, either you have found a real difference about your event, or you have left something out. Both are worth knowing before you sign.
4. **Add contingency last, not first.** Contingency is not spare money for a nicer stage. It is the money that absorbs the thing you have not thought of yet.
5. **Re-check the budget every time a contract is signed.** The signed number replaces the guess, every time.

**About the percentages.** The guides in this template are **rules of thumb, not research**. They come from how conference budgets usually break down, not from a study, and no two events are the same. They are here to start a question — *why is my catering line at 40 percent?* — not to be met exactly. Treat a line that is far outside its range as a prompt to check, not a mistake.

## The percentage guide at a glance

| Line | Rule-of-thumb share of total | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Venue and facility rental | 18–25% | What does the room cost, all in? |
| Catering and beverage | 20–30% | What does feeding people cost, all in? |
| Speaker fees and honoraria | 8–15% | What are we paying the people on stage? |
| Speaker travel and lodging | 4–8% | What does getting them there cost? |
| Musicians and music leadership | 2–5% | Who is leading the music, and what do they cost? |
| Sound, AV and staging | 5–10% | Can the room hear and see? |
| Backline and equipment rental | 2–4% | What gear are we renting rather than bringing? |
| Printing, signage and décor | 3–6% | What does the room look and read like? |
| Registration materials and badges | 1–3% | What does the front desk hand over? |
| Marketing and promotion | 3–8% | How does anyone find out about this? |
| Staff and volunteers | 3–6% | Who is working, and are they paid? |
| Insurance, permits and licenses | 1–4% | What must exist before the doors open? |
| Contingency | 5–10% | What absorbs the surprise? |

These ranges do not add up to 100 percent on their own, because each one is a range. The planning column below is what they add up to if you take a middle value for each line, and it is what the worksheet uses.

## Fixed costs and per-person costs

The single most useful thing to understand about a conference budget is that some lines do not care how many people come.

- **Fixed lines** cost roughly the same at 100 seats as at 500: venue rental, AV and staging, the speaker fee, insurance, most printing.
- **Per-person lines** scale with headcount: catering, badges and registration materials, chairs, printed programs, sometimes venue service charges.

This is why a 100 seat event feels so much more expensive per person than a 500 seat event: the fixed lines are spread across a fifth as many people. Before you set a registration price, divide your fixed lines by your expected headcount — that is the price of being in the room before anyone eats.

A rough per-person check: add your per-person lines, divide by headcount, and compare the result with your registration fee. If the fee does not cover the per-person lines, every additional attendee makes the event more expensive, and you need another income line to make that work.

## The lines, one by one

### 1. Venue and facility rental

**What it typically covers:** the main meeting room; breakout or workshop rooms; lobby, foyer or gathering space; set-up and tear-down days if you need them; tables, chairs and basic linens; parking or access arrangements; sometimes a room block or minimum spend instead of a flat fee.

**What people forget:**
- **The difference between the room and the day.** Many venues price by day, and a conference usually needs the day before for set-up. Ask what the second day costs before you assume it is included.
- **Room minimums.** Some venues charge a low rental and a high minimum food and beverage spend. If you do not hit the minimum, you pay the difference anyway.
- **Service charges and gratuities** that are added to food and beverage, not quoted with the rental.
- **Overtime and early access.** Arriving at 6 a.m. when the contract says 8 a.m. is often a charge, not a courtesy.
- **Cleanup and damage.** Who clears the room, who pays if a wall is scuffed, what the deposit covers.
- **The hold that expires.** A venue may hold your date for a week and release it without telling you.

**Sanity check:** 18–25 percent of total. If venue and catering together are under 40 percent, check that you have not left the service charge out of both.

### 2. Catering and beverage

**What it typically covers:** coffee and something to eat on arrival; mid-morning and mid-afternoon breaks; lunch; water stations; any reception or evening meal; dietary meals; staffing; rentals for serving; and the service charge and gratuity on top.

**What people forget:**
- **The service charge and gratuity are usually on top** of the per-person price, and they are often the two lines that break the budget. Ask for the all-in number per person in writing.
- **Dietary meals are usually counted separately** and sometimes priced per meal rather than per person.
- **Coffee runs out.** One urn per hundred people is not enough for a morning break; ask how many gallons or urns are planned.
- **Volunteers and staff eat too**, and they are often not in the headcount you gave the caterer.
- **Beverage minimums and corkage.** If you bring your own coffee or wine, what does the venue charge for the privilege?
- **The 20 percent you do not know about yet.** Registrations always move in the last two weeks; ask what the caterer charges for a headcount increase, and what the deadline is for the final number.
- **Food that has to be thrown away** if the final count is too high. Ask what happens if you guarantee 300 and 240 attend.

**Sanity check:** 20–30 percent of total. This is usually the largest single line. If it is much lower than venue, confirm the service charge is really included.

### 3. Speaker fees and honoraria

**What it typically covers:** the fee for each keynote or plenary speaker; workshop or breakout leaders; a panel moderator; a host or emcee if they are paid; and an honorarium for a speaker who is not charging a commercial fee.

**What people forget:**
- **A "no fee" speaker is rarely free.** Travel, lodging, meals and a gift are still costs, and they belong somewhere in the budget even if not on this line.
- **Multiple sessions, one fee.** Confirm whether the fee covers one talk or the whole day. A second session is often a second fee.
- **Panel members** are often forgotten because they are local.
- **The honorarium is not the gift.** Many organizers budget a gift, a plaque or a thank-you dinner and leave it out entirely.
- **Payment timing.** Some speakers want half on booking. That is cash out months before any registration money arrives.

**Sanity check:** 8–15 percent of total. On a 100 seat event, expect the top of that range or above, because a fixed fee is a bigger share of a smaller total.

**Deciding the number itself is a page of its own:** [What to pay a guest speaker](/resources/speaker-fees-and-honorariums) covers what drives a fee, what to ask the speaker's office, how travel is kept separate, and a worksheet that turns it into one figure.

### 4. Speaker travel and lodging

**What it typically covers:** flights or ground transport; airport transfers; hotel nights before and after the event; meals while traveling; parking; and sometimes a per diem.

**What people forget:**
- **Who books.** If the speaker books and invoices you, you may pay a marked-up fare. If you book, you own the change fees.
- **The night before is not optional** for a morning keynote. A speaker arriving at 9 a.m. for a 9:30 session is one delayed flight away from an empty stage.
- **Extra nights** for a speaker who arrives early to record or to attend.
- **Baggage, seat selection and ground transport** add up, and nobody puts them in the first draft.
- **The cap.** Agree a maximum reimbursement in writing, or you will be asked for a business class seat eventually.

**Sanity check:** 4–8 percent of total. If it is near zero, you have probably assumed speakers cover their own travel — confirm that in writing, because many do not.

### 5. Musicians and music leadership

**What it typically covers:** the person or team leading music; instrumentalists; vocalists; a rehearsal or sound check; travel and lodging for the music team; and any licensing for music that is performed or played.

**What people forget:**
- **Rehearsal time is paid time**, and it often happens the day before.
- **Each additional musician is another fee and another split.** A five piece band is five negotiations, not one.
- **Gear is not included in the fee.** Backline is a separate line below.
- **Music played between sessions or from a laptop** may need a license. Check what the venue already covers.
- **Who cues the music** and whether that person is a volunteer with a phone or a paid operator.

**Sanity check:** 2–5 percent of total. If your event has a large music program, treat that range as too low rather than too high.

### 6. Sound, AV and staging

**What it typically covers:** microphones (handheld, lavalier and podium); a mixing desk and an operator; speakers and monitors; projectors, screens and confidence monitors; laptops and playback; lighting for the stage; a stage or riser; cabling; and a technician on site for the whole day.

**What people forget:**
- **The operator is a person, and the person costs money.** A room full of rented equipment with nobody at the desk is a room full of silence.
- **Confidence monitors and a timer for the speaker** are small costs that change how a session feels.
- **The venue's "house AV"** may be included, may be basic, and may come with a technician charge. Ask which.
- **Laptop switching between sessions** is where most conferences lose five minutes. Budget for a switcher or a second laptop.
- **Recording.** If you plan to record or livestream, that is usually a separate package and a separate cost.
- **Hearing assistance and accessibility** equipment may be required by the venue's own rules.

**Sanity check:** 5–10 percent of total. If AV is under 3 percent, ask whether an operator is actually included.

### 7. Backline and equipment rental

**What it typically covers:** instruments and amplifiers for the music team; drum kit; keyboards; music stands; extra microphones; a piano or organ if the room has one worth using; a riser for the band; and delivery, set-up and collection.

**What people forget:**
- **Delivery and collection are usually extra**, and they often happen outside the venue's load-in window.
- **Who sets it up.** Rental companies deliver to the door; someone has to carry it and place it.
- **Who is responsible if it is damaged**, and whether your insurance or theirs covers it.
- **The rehearsal needs the gear too**, not just the performance.
- **Small things**: cables, stands, power strips, gaffer tape. Cheap individually, and always missing.

**Sanity check:** 2–4 percent of total. If the music team is bringing its own gear, this line may be near zero — but confirm, because "we have our own" often means "we have some of it".

### 8. Printing, signage and décor

**What it typically covers:** printed programs or handouts; banners and backdrops; direction signs; room and session signs; name cards for the front table; table numbers; welcome signage at the entrance; and simple décor for the stage or the gathering space.

**What people forget:**
- **Revisions cost money.** A program that changes twice is three print runs unless you agree on a proof deadline.
- **Signage has to be designed before it can be printed**, and that is either a designer's fee or your own weekend.
- **Easels, stands and frames** to hold the signs, and who owns them.
- **The last-minute sign** for a room change, which nobody budgets and everybody needs.
- **Where the signs go** — many venues restrict what can be taped or hung, and some charge for mounting.

**Sanity check:** 3–6 percent of total. If this line is zero, you are planning to print at home the night before. Sometimes that is fine; decide it on purpose.

### 9. Registration materials and badges

**What it typically covers:** name badges and holders; lanyards; wristbands or tickets; a printed attendee list; bags or folders if you are giving them; pens and notebooks; and the materials for the front desk itself.

**What people forget:**
- **Spare badges.** Order for your expected headcount plus a margin, because walk-ins and replacements happen.
- **Badge printing on site** costs more than badges printed in advance, and needs a printer and someone to run it.
- **The volunteer kit** at the desk: scissors, tape, a stapler, spare pens, a clipboard, a printed list, and a phone charger.
- **Signs for the desk** so people know which line is the right line.
- **Something to do with the leftover badges** — they carry names on them, so they cannot simply be binned casually.

**Sanity check:** 1–3 percent of total. Small, but it is the line people cut first and regret on the morning.

### 10. Marketing and promotion

**What it typically covers:** the registration page or website; design for a flyer or a social image; printed invitations or mailers; any paid promotion; a photographer or videographer if they are there to produce promotion material; and the cost of whatever you use to collect registrations and take payment.

**What people forget:**
- **Payment processing fees** are a real cost line. Whoever takes the money keeps a percentage; confirm who absorbs it.
- **The time cost of promotion.** Someone has to write, design, post and answer questions, and that is usually the same person running the event.
- **Print deadlines.** A mailer that arrives after the registration deadline is money spent for nothing.
- **Follow-up material**: a reminder email, a final call, a thank-you note. All cheap, all forgotten.
- **Photography** is often budgeted as marketing and then cut, and it is the only record you will have.

**Sanity check:** 3–8 percent of total. A first-year event usually sits at the top of this range, because nobody knows about it yet.

### 11. Staff and volunteers

**What it typically covers:** any paid event staff or coordinator time; a venue event manager if charged separately; security or parking attendants if required; childcare workers; and the real cost of volunteers — meals, a shirt or badge, transport, and a thank-you.

**What people forget:**
- **Volunteers are not free.** They eat, they need a shirt or a badge so attendees can find them, and they need a briefing session before the day.
- **Someone has to manage the volunteers**, and that person cannot also be at the front desk.
- **Childcare** is a cost and sometimes a legal requirement if you advertise it.
- **Security or crowd management** may be required by the venue or the fire code for your size.
- **The person who does the work after the event** — invoices, thank-yous, refunds — is a cost even when they are a volunteer.

**Sanity check:** 3–6 percent of total. If this line is zero, you are relying entirely on goodwill. Say so out loud and plan for it.

### 12. Insurance, permits and licenses

**What it typically covers:** event liability insurance, either a one-off policy or a rider on an existing one; any permit the venue or the city requires; music licensing; and food handling requirements if you are serving anything yourselves.

**What people forget:**
- **The venue will ask for a certificate of insurance**, often weeks before the event, and often with a specific wording and a named additional insured.
- **Cover for the equipment you rented**, which may need to be added.
- **Cancellation or weather cover**, if it is available to you and you want it.
- **Permits take time.** A late application is a fee or a refusal, and neither is in the budget.
- **Who is covered.** Volunteers and contractors may not be, and the gap only appears after something happens.

**Sanity check:** 1–4 percent of total. Small line, but the one that can stop the event. Confirm the requirement in writing rather than assuming your existing policy covers it.

### 13. Contingency

**What it typically covers:** nothing, until something happens. It absorbs the price that rose, the headcount that grew, the speaker who needed a second night, the sign nobody planned.

**What people forget:**
- **Contingency is not a discount you spend.** If you spend it on a nicer stage in week one, you have none left in week twelve.
- **It should be a percentage of the total, set at the start** and protected.
- **It should have an owner.** Someone has to decide when it is released and record what it was spent on.
- **It does not replace a deposit.** You still need cash on hand for deposits before registrations arrive.

**Sanity check:** 5–10 percent of total. A first-year event, or one with a venue you have not used, belongs at the top of the range.

## Cash flow: money leaves before it arrives

A budget can balance and still leave you unable to pay a deposit in March.

The order of money in most conferences:

1. **Venue deposit** — usually due when you sign, often months before registration opens.
2. **Speaker deposit** — sometimes due on booking, sometimes not until the event.
3. **Catering deposit** — often due 30 to 60 days out, with the balance due after the event or on the day.
4. **AV, rental, printing deposits** — spread across the last two months.
5. **Registration income** — arrives mostly in the final weeks, and often in the final days.

So the gap is real: you may be paying deposits from your own funds, or from a sponsor, or from an early-bird push. Write down, for each contract, **when** the money leaves, not just how much. If you have a treasurer, a board or a finance committee, that date list is what they need to see.

Three practical habits:

- **Ask for the payment schedule as a schedule.** Every vendor has one; most organizers only get the total.
- **Keep a separate line for processing fees** if you are taking card payments, and know who pays them.
- **Never spend a deposit you have not collected.** Count registration income as money you have, not money you expect.
