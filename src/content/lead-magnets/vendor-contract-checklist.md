# Vendor Contract Checklist

What to confirm in writing before you sign for a venue or a speaker.

Two contracts decide whether your conference goes well: the room and the person on stage. Both are usually signed months before anything else is final, and both are usually signed in a hurry, in a meeting, on a document someone else wrote. This checklist is the list of questions to get answered before you sign.

Print it. Take it to the meeting. If an answer is "we'll work that out later", write it in the contract anyway, because later never arrives with the same generosity.

---

## Before you sign anything

- [ ] **You are signing as the right entity.** The contract should name the organization that is actually paying, not you personally. If you sign personally, you are personally liable.
- [ ] **The person across the table can commit.** Ask whether they are authorized to sign, or whether someone else has to countersign. A signature from someone who cannot bind the organization is worth nothing.
- [ ] **Everything is in writing.** A phone call, a hallway promise and a verbal discount do not exist after the staff member who made them leaves. Send a short email after every call: *"Confirming what we agreed today…"* — that email becomes evidence.
- [ ] **You are reading the current version.** Ask for the contract as a document, not a photograph of a screen, and check the date on it.
- [ ] **You have the schedule, not just the price.** Every vendor has a payment schedule and a cancellation schedule. Ask for both as a page, not a sentence.
- [ ] **You have the floor plan or the rider.** For a venue, a plan with your layout drawn on it. For a speaker, their technical and travel requirements.
- [ ] **You know what happens if you walk away.** Understand the cancellation terms before you sign, because that is the clause you cannot negotiate later.

---

## The venue: confirm in writing

The room is a physical object, and the questions below assume you have already stood in it. If you have not toured the venue yet, the [venue site visit checklist](/resources/venue-site-visit-checklist) is the hour before this one: what to bring, what to measure, what to photograph, and the numbers to ask for while you are standing there. Visit first, then confirm it in writing.

### 1. What "seated capacity" actually means

This is the number most often misunderstood, and it is the one that decides whether you can sell 400 registrations.

Venue capacity depends entirely on **how the room is set**, and venues quote the most generous number they can.

| Layout | What it means | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| Theater / auditorium | Rows of chairs facing one direction | The highest number, but no tables and no group work |
| Classroom | Rows of tables with chairs facing forward | Fewer seats than theater; good for note-taking |
| Banquet / rounds | Tables of eight or ten | Usually the lowest number, and the layout most events actually use |
| Standing / reception | No seating | Not a conference capacity; be careful comparing it |
| Cabaret / half-rounds | Chairs around tables, angled | Between banquet and theater |

Ask for these specifics, in writing:

- [ ] **Which layout does the quoted number describe?** Get the number per layout, not one number.
- [ ] **Is that the fire code occupancy or the venue's own comfortable number?** These are different, and the fire number is a legal maximum, not a good experience.
- [ ] **Does the number include the space the stage, sound desk and screens take?** A stage and a front-of-house position can take a meaningful bite out of the seats.
- [ ] **What does it drop to with your actual layout**, including aisles, a registration table at the back, and camera or AV positions?
- [ ] **Get a plan with your layout drawn on it, and count the seats yourself.** This is the single most useful thing you can do. If the count on the plan is lower than the number in the contract, that conversation happens now, not in the week before.
- [ ] **What is the maximum if the room is set for a meal** and you still need people to see the stage?

### 2. Dates, holds and the room you actually get

- [ ] **The date, the start and end times, and the day before for set-up.** Written down, including the hour access begins.
- [ ] **Whether the hold expires**, and when. A hold that lapses quietly is how a date is lost.
- [ ] **Which specific rooms are included**, by name. "Main hall plus two breakouts" is not a contract term; "Hall A plus Rooms 3 and 4" is.
- [ ] **What happens if the venue moves you.** Renovation, a bigger booking, a double-booking — can they reassign your space or your date, and what do you get if they do? Ask for that in writing, including a right to cancel without penalty if the replacement space does not seat your number.
- [ ] **Whether another event is booked in the same building at the same time**, and whether that affects parking, noise or the lobby.

### 3. Load-in and load-out windows

- [ ] **The exact window** you can bring equipment in, and the exact window to take it out. Not "the day before" — a time.
- [ ] **What happens if you run over.** Overtime rates, and who authorizes them.
- [ ] **Where you load in.** Which door, which elevator, which corridor, and whether the route fits a large case or a piano.
- [ ] **Whether there is a freight elevator**, and whether it needs booking or an operator.
- [ ] **Cart, dolly and labor availability** — can you borrow equipment, or do you bring it?
- [ ] **Parking for your team and for the speaker**, and whether it is free, validated or charged.
- [ ] **Storage** for empty cases and boxes during the event, and who is responsible for them.

### 4. AV, power and rigging

- [ ] **What AV is included in the rental** — microphones, a projector, a screen, a sound system, a technician — and what is charged separately.
- [ ] **Whether you may bring your own AV**, or whether the venue requires its own provider. Some venues do, and that changes your budget.
- [ ] **Power**: how many circuits, where they are, and whether the room's power is enough for your sound and lighting without tripping.
- [ ] **Rigging and hanging**: may you hang anything from the ceiling, and does that need a licensed rigger? This is often a separate fee and sometimes a refusal.
- [ ] **Internet**: wired or wireless, how much bandwidth, whether it is shared with the rest of the building, and whether a dedicated line costs extra. If you are livestreaming or running an app, this matters.
- [ ] **Lighting** in the room, and whether it can be dimmed during sessions.
- [ ] **A technician on site for the whole day**, with a name and a rate, not "someone will be around".
- [ ] **Sound limits** — is there a noise restriction or a curfew, and what time does it start?

### 5. Insurance and liability

- [ ] **What certificate of insurance the venue requires**, with the exact wording and the exact named insured, and how many days before the event it is due.
- [ ] **Whether your existing policy covers events**, or whether you need a one-off policy.
- [ ] **Who is liable for what** — injury in the room, damage to the building, loss of your equipment, loss of a speaker's equipment.
- [ ] **Whether the venue's insurance covers your rented equipment** (it usually does not), and what you need to add.
- [ ] **Whether volunteers and contractors are covered.**
- [ ] **Whether the venue requires a security guard or crowd management** at your attendance level, and who pays.
- [ ] **What the damage deposit covers**, what it does not, and when it comes back.

### 6. Deposit and payment schedule

- [ ] **The deposit amount and the date it is due** — usually on signature.
- [ ] **Whether the deposit is refundable**, and until when.
- [ ] **The full schedule**: what is due at signing, at 90 days, at 30 days, on the day, and after.
- [ ] **What the final invoice includes** — service charges, gratuities, overtime, cleanup, and anything else that gets added at the end.
- [ ] **Who absorbs card processing fees**, if you are paying by card.
- [ ] **What happens if a payment is late** — interest, a cancelled booking, or a released date.
- [ ] **Who the invoice comes from**, and the exact legal name and address for your records.

### 7. Cancellation and force majeure

Read this section twice. It is the one that costs the most.

- [ ] **If you cancel**, what do you lose at each stage — 90 days out, 30 days, a week, the day before? Get the ladder, not a sentence.
- [ ] **If the venue cancels**, what do you get? A refund is the minimum; ask for the cost of finding another room.
- [ ] **What counts as force majeure** — weather, illness, travel disruption, a government order — and does it protect both sides or only one?
- [ ] **Whether a force majeure clause requires you to reschedule first** rather than cancel, and what that costs if you cannot.
- [ ] **Whether there is a "liquidated damages" clause**, which can mean you owe a set amount or the whole contract value, not just the deposit.
- [ ] **What happens if attendance is far below your estimate** and you need a smaller room — can you downsize, and what does that cost?

### 8. Who pays for what

Print this, fill in the third column with the venue, and get it attached to the contract.

| Item | Usually one side's responsibility | In our contract |
|---|---|---|
| Room rental, tables, chairs, linens | Venue | |
| Set-up and tear-down labor | Often venue, sometimes extra | |
| Service charge and gratuity | Almost always on top of food | |
| Cleanup during and after | Usually venue, confirm | |
| Extra cleaning for confetti, food, décor | Almost always you | |
| Food and beverage | Usually venue, often exclusive | |
| Outside food, cake, or coffee | Often restricted or surcharged | |
| Water stations | Often overlooked, sometimes charged | |
| AV, microphones, projector, screen | Sometimes included, usually extra | |
| AV technician | Almost always extra | |
| Power and rigging | Often extra | |
| Internet, dedicated line | Usually extra | |
| Signage and hanging | Often restricted, sometimes charged | |
| Parking and valet | Usually extra | |
| Security, if required | Often you | |
| Insurance certificate | You | |
| Damage and loss | You, against the deposit | |
| Overtime | You | |
| Shipping and storage of your materials | You, confirm storage is free | |

The two lines that surprise people most are **service charge and gratuity** on food and beverage, and **"additional charges"** — a general clause that lets a venue add costs later. Ask for the list of what can be added, and ask for it capped.

### 9. Catering exclusivity and outside food

- [ ] **Is catering exclusive to the venue?** If yes, you cannot shop around, and you should negotiate the food price knowing that.
- [ ] **May you bring your own coffee, water, snacks or a celebration cake**, and is there a charge (corkage, plating, a service fee)?
- [ ] **The final headcount deadline**, and what happens if you need to raise it after that date.
- [ ] **What happens if the actual attendance is below the guarantee** — do you pay for the guarantee or the actual?
- [ ] **Dietary meals**: how they are counted and priced, and how they are identified on the day.
- [ ] **Whether the venue staffs the meal**, or whether you provide servers.

## The speaker: confirm in writing

A speaker agreement does not need to be long. It needs to be specific. A one-page email that both sides reply "agreed" to is far better than nothing.
The conversation starts before the contract, with the invitation itself: the [speaker invitation letter template](/resources/speaker-invitation-letter) has three letters you can send today, and they settle the fee, the travel, the recording and the cancellation question before anyone signs anything. Once those answers exist, the [speaker contract template](/resources/speaker-contract-template) is where they get written down — eighteen clauses, every blank named, ready to fill in and sign.

### 1. The fee and what it includes

- [ ] **The fee**, in figures, and the currency.
- [ ] **How many sessions it covers**, and the length of each. A second session is often a second fee; agree that now.
- [ ] **Whether Q&A, a panel, a workshop or a book signing is included** or extra.
- [ ] **Whether the fee includes preparation and materials**, or whether handouts and slides are extra.
- [ ] **Whether there is a separate fee for recording or streaming** the session.

### 2. Payment schedule

- [ ] **When the deposit is due** and whether it is refundable.
- [ ] **When the balance is due** — before the event, on the day, or on invoice after.
- [ ] **Who pays card or transfer fees.**
- [ ] **The invoice details** the speaker needs, and who on your side approves payment.
- [ ] **Whether the fee is stated as inclusive or exclusive of any tax**, and who is responsible for it. If you do not know your own tax position, ask your treasurer or accountant — do not guess on a contract.

### 3. Travel and lodging

- [ ] **Who books the travel** — the speaker or you. Whoever books owns the change fees.
- [ ] **The class of travel and the maximum you will reimburse.** Set a cap in writing or you will be asked for more.
- [ ] **How many nights of lodging you cover**, and which nights. The night before a morning session is standard for a reason.
- [ ] **Whether ground transport, parking, baggage and meals are reimbursed**, and at what rate.
- [ ] **Whether reimbursement is against receipts**, and by when receipts are due.
- [ ] **What happens if the speaker's travel is disrupted** and they need another night.

### 4. What they will actually deliver

- [ ] **The session title, the length and the start time**, and the room.
- [ ] **What equipment they need** — a specific microphone, a piano, a particular connector for a laptop, a clicker. Get it in writing and pass it to your AV team.
- [ ] **Whether they will send slides or materials**, by when, and in what format.
- [ ] **Whether they will attend a rehearsal or a sound check**, and when.
- [ ] **Whether they will stay for the whole event** or arrive for their session and leave. Both are fine; you need to know which, because it changes the program and the hospitality plan.
- [ ] **Whether they will promote the event** to their own audience, and what you will give them to do it.

### 5. Recording, photography and use of their name

- [ ] **Who owns the recording** of the session, and whether you may post it, sell it, or use clips.
- [ ] **Whether you may photograph them** and use the images in future promotion.
- [ ] **Whether they may record or film themselves** at your event, and post it.
- [ ] **Whether their name and photo may be used in your marketing** before and after the event.
- [ ] **Whether the fee changes if you record.** Some speakers charge more for it; find out now, not after the cameras are set up.

### 6. Products, books and selling from the stage

- [ ] **Whether they may sell books or products** at the event, and where.
- [ ] **Whether they may promote their own programs, courses or consulting** from the stage, and how much of the session that may take.
- [ ] **Whether you get a share of sales**, or a discount on books for attendees. Ask; sometimes the answer is yes.
- [ ] **Whether they will bring stock**, or whether you order it, and who pays shipping.

### 7. If the speaker cancels, and if you cancel

- [ ] **If the speaker cancels**, what happens to the deposit and the balance? Get it in writing that the deposit is returned if they cancel without a good reason.
- [ ] **What counts as a good reason** — illness, a family emergency, travel disruption — and who decides.
- [ ] **Whether they will help find a replacement**, or record a session, if they cannot come.
- [ ] **If you cancel**, what do you owe? A cancellation close to the date often means the full fee.
- [ ] **If you reschedule rather than cancel**, does the fee carry over, and does the date have to be within a certain window?
- [ ] **Whether the speaker has an out if a lower attendance threshold is not met**, and what that threshold is.

### 8. Exclusivity and conflicts

- [ ] **Whether they will speak at a competing or nearby event** in the weeks around yours, and whether you care.
- [ ] **Whether they are speaking for another organization in your area** on the same trip — often fine, sometimes awkward, always worth knowing.
- [ ] **Whether they will mention your event publicly** before the date.

## The three clauses people most often regret

**1. The cancellation clause that only protects one side.**
The version people sign most often says the deposit is non-refundable if you cancel, but says nothing about what happens if the venue or speaker cancels. Read the two halves separately and check that both exist. Watch for "liquidated damages", which can mean you owe a set amount or the whole contract value rather than just the deposit, and watch for a force majeure clause that covers only the other party. If weather, illness or a government order would stop your event, that protection should work in both directions.

**2. The clause that lets them move your date or your room.**
A venue that can reassign your space for a renovation or a larger booking, or a speaker who can move to a different date with no penalty, leaves you holding the marketing, the deposits and the registrations. If the clause exists, negotiate a right to cancel without penalty when the replacement is not equivalent — and define "equivalent" by seats, not by description. The same shape appears in speaker agreements as a broad right to reschedule.

**3. The recording and likeness clause, or its absence.**
If nothing is written, you probably cannot post the session, and the speaker may be able to use footage of your event however they like. Decide who owns the recording, whether you may publish it, whether you may use clips and photographs in future promotion, and whether the fee covers recording at all. This is the cheapest clause to fix before signing and the most expensive to fix afterwards, because after the event the recording already exists and the leverage is gone.

**Honorable mention — the open door.** A line like "additional charges may apply" or "services billed as incurred" is not a term, it is a permission. Ask for the list of what can be added and a cap on it, or you will meet the venue's definition of "reasonable" on the final invoice.

## The email to send before you sign

Copy this, adjust it, and send it. A written reply is worth more than a good conversation.

> Thank you for the draft. Before we sign, could you confirm the following in writing:
>
> 1. The seated capacity for our layout, and a floor plan with that layout drawn on it.
> 2. The exact access, load-in and load-out times, including the day before.
> 3. What is included in the rental price, and the full list of items that are charged separately — including service charge, gratuity, overtime, cleanup, power, rigging and internet.
> 4. The complete payment schedule with dates, and whether the deposit is refundable and until when.
> 5. The cancellation terms for both sides, and what counts as force majeure.
> 6. The insurance certificate requirements, the wording, and the date it is due.
> 7. Whether we may bring our own AV and our own food or beverage, and any charge for doing so.
>
> If any of these is not settled yet, we are happy to wait — we would rather have it in the contract than agreed by phone.

---

## One page to take into the meeting

- Seats, by layout, on a plan you have counted yourself.
- Access times, in hours.
- What is included, and what is extra, as a list.
- Deposit, refundability, and the full payment schedule with dates.
- Cancellation terms for both sides.
- Insurance wording and due date.
- Who pays for each line in the table above.
- What happens if they cancel, move you, or go over on time.

If you cannot fill in every line, you are not ready to sign.
