# Conference planning committee roles and responsibilities

A conference planning committee rarely fails because the work is too hard. It fails because two people are quietly doing most of it, a third is handling the money without telling anyone, and nobody is sure who is allowed to say yes to a vendor. By the time that becomes visible, a date has been announced in public and a vendor is expecting a payment.

This page is about the people rather than the calendar. It sets out the eight seats a conference committee actually has, what each one owns, the decisions each one is allowed to make alone, and where the money and the contracts sit. It is written to be read once by a committee chair and then passed to the rest of the committee, so that everyone is working from the same description of their own job — and so that a volunteer who steps down can be replaced without the event losing its memory.

It is also written for the person who has just been handed a committee that already exists — eight people, a date, and no description of what any of them is for. You do not have to reorganize anything to use it. Read the eight seats below, write the names you already have beside the ones that match, and notice which seats are empty. That is usually where the trouble is, and it is much cheaper to find out in month twelve than in month four.

**What this page does not do.** Our [conference planning checklist](/resources/conference-planning-checklist) owns the twelve-month calendar — when each thing has to be finished. The [conference day timeline](/resources/conference-day-timeline) owns the last month and the day itself. The [budget template](/resources/conference-budget-template) owns the cost lines, the [vendor contract checklist](/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) owns the terms to get in writing, the [venue site visit checklist](/resources/venue-site-visit-checklist) owns the hour you spend in the room, and the [speaker fee guide](/resources/speaker-fees-and-honorariums) owns the number you offer. This page owns the question all of those assume has been answered: **who**. If the seats below have no names against them yet, start here, then go back to the calendar.

## The eight seats, on one page

Eight is enough to cover a conference without inventing work for people to look busy with. Conference planning committee roles and responsibilities get much clearer the moment each role has a decision attached to it, which is why the table below has three columns and not one. A larger event may add seats and a smaller one will combine them — there is a section further down for a committee of three. What does not change is that every one of the eight has exactly one name against it, even when one person's name appears three times.

| Seat | What it owns, in one line | The decision it makes alone |
|---|---|---|
| **Chair** | The committee itself: the meetings, the decision log, the tie-break, and the answer to "who decided that?" | When the committee meets, what is on the agenda, and who is asked to take an empty seat |
| **Program chair** | What happens on the stage: the sessions, the speakers, and the shape of the day | Which proposal is accepted, and the running order |
| **Venue and logistics lead** | The room and the physical event: the site visit, the floor plan, AV, catering, load-in, signage, access routes | The layout inside the rooms you hold, and which supplier you use from the approved list |
| **Finance and contracts lead** | The money and the paper: the budget, the account, the deposits, the contracts, the invoices, the reconciliation | Whether an invoice against a signed contract is paid, and on which date |
| **Registration and attendee care lead** | The attendee list, and everything an attendee experiences before they arrive and at the door | A refund or a transfer inside the rule you have already published |
| **Marketing and communications lead** | Who hears about the event, when, in what words, and through which channel | The wording, the channel and the order of the messages |
| **Volunteer coordinator** | How many volunteers are needed, what each one does, and that each one is briefed and fed | Which volunteer is on which shift, and what the briefing says |
| **Sponsorship and partnerships lead** | The money that is not registration: sponsors, partners, in-kind offers, and what was promised in return | Which prospect to approach next, and whether an offer is worth pursuing |

Read the third column carefully, because it is the column committees skip. Most committees are willing to say what someone is responsible for. Very few say what that person may decide without asking, and the result is that every decision gets escalated to the same two people — who are then, by definition, the two people doing everything.

**A seat with no name is not a seat.** Fill in the third column of your own copy before the first meeting. A seat that belongs to "the committee" is a seat nobody opens, and it will be empty on the day it matters.

## Three rules that decide whether the seats work

The eight seats are a structure. These three rules are what make the structure hold when the year gets busy, and all three are easier to agree in month twelve than to impose in month four. A written list of event planning committee roles is only a description; these three rules are what turn it into an agreement people can hold each other to.

### Rule one: one name per seat, and the name goes on the sheet

Not "the committee", not "whoever is around", not two people "sharing" it. Shared ownership is the single most reliable way to leave a task untouched for a month while four people each assume somebody else has it. If a job genuinely needs two people — a contract that Finance signs and the Chair has to approve — name the one who must make sure it happens, and write the other person in the note.

The seat titles stay the same from year to year. The names do not. Keep a printed seat sheet with the titles already on it and write the current name and cell phone number beside each one by hand, so that when a volunteer steps down you reprint one page instead of rebuilding the plan. There is a blank version at the end of this page.

### Rule two: whoever can say yes can also say no, up to a written amount

For every seat, write down two things: what it may commit without asking, and what it must bring to the committee. Do this in dollars, not in adjectives.

| Seat | May commit alone, up to | Must bring to the committee |
|---|---|---|
| Chair | $ | |
| Program chair | $ | |
| Venue and logistics lead | $ | |
| Finance and contracts lead | $ | |
| Registration and attendee care lead | $ | |
| Marketing and communications lead | $ | |
| Volunteer coordinator | $ | |
| Sponsorship and partnerships lead | $ | |

Fill this in once, at the start, and give every seat a copy. If the answer is "we will work it out when it comes up", it will come up in a parking lot with a vendor waiting, and the person who happens to be standing there will decide. That is how an event acquires a lighting charge that nobody approved and nobody can explain at the debrief.

The corollary matters just as much: **a seat that cannot commit anything is a seat that will stop volunteering.** If a coordinator has to ask before buying tape, they will stop asking and start paying for small things themselves, and the committee will never see the cost.

### Rule three: the seat that spends is not the seat that pays

The Program chair negotiates with a speaker. The venue lead negotiates with the room. Neither of them releases the money, and neither of them signs. The Finance and contracts lead does both, against a budget line the committee already approved. That separation is not distrust — it is what lets a volunteer say "I need to check the budget" to a vendor without it sounding like a personal hesitation.

It also means the person who owns the money is never the only person who has ever looked at it. One reader per seat is enough. One reader in total is how an event discovers a shortfall in the final month.

## The eight seats in detail

Each seat below has the same four parts: what it owns, what it decides alone, what it must bring to someone else, and what it must not do even though it could. The fourth part is the one people find useful, because most committee friction comes from someone doing a job well that was not theirs.

**If you are working from our [conference planning checklist](/resources/conference-planning-checklist) at the same time,** its lines are assigned to seven roles and two of them are named differently here: its Event lead is this Chair, and its Venue liaison is this Venue and logistics lead. The eighth seat is the one a checklist can leave out and a committee cannot — sponsorship, which is the only seat that brings in money that is not registration. Same work, one extra hand.

### 1. Chair

**Owns:** the committee's mandate and its calendar of decisions — what has to be decided, by when, and by whom. The Chair runs the meetings, keeps the decision log, and holds the relationship with whoever the committee answers to, whether that is a board, a director, a principal or a membership.

**Decides alone:** when the committee meets and what is on the agenda; which decision is deferred to the next meeting; who is asked to take an empty seat; and the tie-break when the committee is split and the rules do not settle it.

**Must bring to the committee:** any change to the date, the price, or the scope of the event. Anything that commits the organization in writing. Any decision that would break a promise already made to an attendee, a sponsor or a venue.

**Must not do:** absorb an empty seat silently. When a seat has no name, the Chair's job is to name it, fill it, or say out loud which work is not going to happen — not to quietly do it at night and let the committee believe the structure is working.

**Hands over:** the decision log, the seat sheet, and the calendar of decisions. Those three documents are the Chair's actual output.

### 2. Program chair

**Owns:** what happens on the stage. The session list, the speakers, the shape of the day — how many sessions, how long each one runs, how many tracks, where the breaks fall — and the deadline for every speaker's title, description and materials. The wording of the invitation and the clause language of the agreement belong to the [speaker invitation letter](/resources/speaker-invitation-letter) and the [speaker contract template](/resources/speaker-contract-template); this seat is the one who decides which name goes on which line.

**Decides alone:** which proposal or session is accepted and which is declined, inside the shape of the day the committee has already agreed; the running order; the session length, within the boundaries set; and whether to chase a speaker again or move on.

**Must bring to the committee:** the fee and anything else with a number in it (that is a budget line, so it goes to Finance before it is offered, not after); any session that adds a room, a track or a day; and any speaker request that the venue has to grant.

**Must not do:** promise a fee, a travel arrangement or a recording right without the money seat and the contract seat agreeing it first. A friendly "we will sort the details out later" on a phone call becomes an obligation the moment the speaker repeats it to someone else.

**Hands over:** the confirmed program with names, titles and times; each speaker's technical requirements, in writing, to the venue lead; and the final running order to whoever runs the day.

### 3. Venue and logistics lead

**Owns:** the physical event. The site visits, the room, the floor plan, the AV, the catering coordination, the load-in and load-out windows, the signage, the access routes, the storage, and the transport plan. The tour itself has its own guide — the [venue site visit checklist](/resources/venue-site-visit-checklist) — because what you measure and count in the room is a different job from who is accountable for the room.

**Decides alone:** the layout inside the rooms you already hold; which supplier you use from the list the committee approved; the placement of signage, registration tables and refreshment points; the order of the load-in within the contracted window.

**Must bring to the committee:** anything that changes the value of the contract — an added room, extra hours, an overtime rate, a service charge, a supplier the venue requires; anything that changes the date or the access times; and any request from the venue that affects the program.

**Must not do:** sign, or pay. This seat negotiates hard and gets the terms in writing, then hands them to Finance. A venue lead who can also sign is a venue lead who will eventually sign something in a hurry.

**Hands over:** a room pack — floor plan, the venue's contact names, the access and load-in times, what is included and what is extra — to the day-of team, and a copy to Finance with the contract.

### 4. Finance and contracts lead

**Owns:** the money and the paper. The budget, the account the money sits in, the deposits, the payment schedule, the signed contracts, the invoices, the reconciliation after the event, and the short written report of what it actually cost. The [budget template](/resources/conference-budget-template) builds the lines; this seat is accountable for them.

**Decides alone:** whether an invoice against a signed contract gets paid, and on which payment date; whether a proposed cost fits inside a line the committee has already approved; how a cost is coded; and refusing a charge that is not in the budget at all.

**Must bring to the committee:** any new cost above the line; anything that would take the total past the ceiling; any contract with a term the committee has not seen, including cancellation, exclusivity and auto-renewal; and the monthly numbers, in the room, on screen.

**Must not do:** approve a new expense and pay it in the same movement without telling anyone. The point of the ceiling is that going past it is a decision somebody else makes, on the record.

**Hands over:** a monthly one-page position — committed, paid, still to pay, and the gap against the ceiling — plus a contract register listing every agreement, its value, its payment dates and where the signed copy is filed.

### 5. Registration and attendee care lead

**Owns:** the attendee list, and everything an attendee experiences from the moment they decide to come. The registration form, the confirmation, the fee mechanics, the refund and transfer rule as it is actually applied, the dietary and accessibility questions, the badges, the printed list, the desk on the day, and the person who answers the reply-to address.

**Decides alone:** whether a refund or a transfer is granted inside the rule the committee has already published; the desk layout and the queue; a badge reprinted on the day; how a dietary or access request is routed to the person who will act on it.

**Must bring to the committee:** any exception to the published rule — a refund after the deadline, a transfer to a different event, a discount for a group; any change to the price or to what the price includes; and any request that has no owner yet, such as a request for childcare or a quiet room.

**Must not do:** invent a policy in a reply. The first person who asks a question you have not answered will get an answer you made up on the spot, and that answer will be quoted back to you by the second person. If the rule does not cover it, the answer is "let me check and come back to you today", and then the committee decides.

**Hands over:** the final attendee list, the dietary list and the accessibility list, each with a named person who will act on it and the date they need it by.

### 6. Marketing and communications lead

**Owns:** who hears about the event, when, in what words, and through which channel. The announcement calendar, the program announcement, the reminders, the partner asks, the copy that goes to other people to share, and the event's own channels.

**Decides alone:** the wording and the channel; the order in which the messages go out; which partner organizations are approached and in what order; whether a message is repeated.

**Must bring to the committee:** any claim about the event that is not yet true — a price, a capacity, a speaker's name, a session title, a refund promise. Nothing goes public until the seat that owns that fact has confirmed it in writing. This one rule prevents most of the public apologies an event ever has to make.

**Must not do:** spend on promotion without an approved line, and never treat a paid boost as a communications decision. It is a budget decision that happens to have a caption.

**Hands over:** the calendar for the rest of the year — what goes out, on which day, in whose name, to whom — and the final copy for anything that gets printed.

### 7. Volunteer coordinator

**Owns:** how many volunteers the event needs, by role and by shift; what each one does; that each one is briefed, fed, thanked and knows where to park and who to call.

**Decides alone:** which volunteer is on which shift; the format and timing of the briefing; whether a volunteer is sent home, moved, or given a different job on the day.

**Must bring to the committee:** any role that needs training, a license, a background check or insurance cover — do not put a volunteer in that role because they were the one who said yes; and any shift that cannot be filled by name.

**Must not do:** recruit against a number nobody has agreed. "We will need about fifteen people" is how an event ends up with twenty-five volunteers, four jobs, and eleven people standing in a corridor being polite.

**Hands over:** the roster by name and shift, each volunteer's arrival time, their position and who to call — printed, one copy each.

### 8. Sponsorship and partnerships lead

**Owns:** the money that is not registration. Sponsor prospects, partner organizations, in-kind offers, and — the part that gets forgotten — the register of what was promised in return, to whom, and by when.

**Decides alone:** which prospect to approach next; whether an offer is worth pursuing; how a pitch is worded, within the sponsorship levels the committee approved.

**Must bring to the committee:** any discount, any exclusivity, any logo placement, any named billing, and anything that creates a deliverable the committee cannot guarantee — a certain attendance, a certain audience, a mention from a speaker. Get those in writing, on the register, before anyone shakes hands.

**Must not do:** promise a deliverable the event cannot control. Verbal sponsorship with a vague deliverable is the most common unpaid invoice in events, and the argument about it happens after the money was already spent.

**Hands over:** the commitment register — what was promised, to whom, by when, who delivers it — with a copy to Finance so the income and the obligation are in the same place.

## Where the money and the contracts sit

This is the part of a committee structure that people nod at and then get wrong, so it is worth stating plainly.

**One seat owns the money. That seat owns the contracts too.** Not two people, not a subcommittee, not the Chair as a backstop. The Finance and contracts lead holds the budget, the account, the deposits, the signed agreements and the reconciliation, and that is deliberate: a contract and the payment against it are the same obligation seen twice, and splitting them across two people is how a committee ends up paying for something nobody can find the agreement for.

**Every other seat reads, and one of them reads regularly.** The Chair sees everything, at every meeting, on screen. The seat that spends against a line sees that line — the Program chair can see the speaker line without asking, the venue lead can see the venue line without asking. What nobody gets is the ability to change a number they do not own.

**Two habits make the rule real.** First, the money owner produces the position monthly and shows it in the meeting rather than answering questions about it afterward. Second, every signed contract goes into one register the moment it is signed, with the value, the payment dates and where the copy lives — because the failure is never "we did not have a contract", it is "we had one and cannot find it".

**If you run the event in Convocate,** this is the part the tool enforces rather than asks for: you invite a committee member as an Editor who can work on the event, or a Viewer who can read it, and a Viewer does not see the money — the server withholds the figures rather than hiding them in the page. That is a fact about the software and not an argument for it; the rule above works on paper, in a shared folder and in a printed register, and it works better than no rule at all.

## Who decides what

Print this table and settle every row in one meeting. If a row has no clear answer for your event, that is the argument you have not had yet.

| Decision | Decides alone | Must be told before it happens | Can stop it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The date | Chair, with the committee's agreement | Everyone | Committee, if the audience's calendar makes it unworkable |
| The budget ceiling | Committee | Finance | Finance, if it cannot be met |
| Going past the ceiling | Committee, on the record | Everyone | Finance |
| Signing a contract | Finance and contracts lead, on an approved line | Chair | Chair |
| Releasing a payment | Finance and contracts lead | Chair | Finance, if the contract does not support it |
| Accepting a speaker | Program chair | Finance (fee), Venue lead (room) | Finance, if there is no line for it |
| A speaker's fee | Finance, on the approved line | Program chair | Finance |
| The registration fee | Committee | Registration, Marketing | Finance |
| A refund inside the published rule | Registration and attendee care lead | Finance | Nobody — it is inside the rule |
| A refund outside the published rule | Committee | Finance | Finance |
| The room layout | Venue and logistics lead | Program chair | Program chair, if it breaks the session plan |
| A public claim about price or capacity | Marketing, once the owning seat confirms it in writing | Committee | The seat that owns the fact |
| Closing registration or opening a waitlist | Committee | Registration, Marketing | Registration, if the room cannot hold it |
| Accepting a sponsor or an in-kind offer | Sponsorship lead, within approved levels | Finance, Chair | Finance, if it creates an obligation |
| Cutting a cost line when the budget is over | Committee | Everyone | Chair |
| Filling an empty seat | Chair | Committee | Nobody |
| Moving a date | Committee | Everyone, including every contracted supplier | Venue, if the contract does not allow it |

Two rows on this table are worth more than the rest. **A refund inside the published rule is not a committee decision** — if a volunteer at a desk has to text three people to approve something the published rule already covers, the rule is decoration. And **a public claim needs the owning seat's written confirmation** — the price is Finance's fact, the capacity is the venue lead's fact, the speaker is the program chair's fact. Marketing publishes; it does not decide what is true.

## The decision log

One document, one line per decision, kept by the Chair and read out at the start of every meeting.

| Date | Decision | Decided by | What it commits us to | Reversible until |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |
| | | | | |

The last column does the most work, and it is the one committees leave out. "Reversible until" is the date after which the decision costs money to change — the day the deposit is non-refundable, the day the program goes to print, the day the caterer needs the final count. Write it when the decision is made, not when it is nearly too late.

The log answers the question that otherwise gets answered by whoever has the loudest memory: **who decided that?** It is also the fastest handover document in the business. A new Chair can read twelve months of decisions in twenty minutes.

## When the committee is three people

Most conferences are not run by eight people. They are run by three, each holding two or three seats. The seats do not change; the combinations do, and three combinations have to be avoided out loud rather than discovered in month five.

| Person | Holds | Must not also hold |
|---|---|---|
| **Person A** | Chair, Program chair | Finance — not alone, and never as the only person who can see the total |
| **Person B** | Finance and contracts, Registration and attendee care | The final say on what gets cut when the budget does not fit |
| **Person C** | Venue and logistics, Marketing and communications | Every public channel, if nobody else can see what is about to go out |
| **Shared, or dropped** | Volunteer coordinator, Sponsorship and partnerships | — |

Three things to decide on day one, before anything else:

**Who breaks a tie.** With three people, two will disagree and the third settles it. Write down who that is, and get the other two to agree that they will accept it. The alternative is a decision that stalls for three weeks at exactly the wrong moment.

**Who can see the money, and how often.** At three people, one person holds Finance and Registration, so the list and the payments sit in one pair of hands. That is workable. What is not workable is that person being the only one who has ever seen the total. Person A looks at the budget once a month, in the room, numbers on screen. Fifteen minutes, twelve times.

**What gets dropped first.** Three people cannot do what eight can. Pick in advance the two or three things you will let go if the year gets hard — the printed program, the second track, the social schedule, the sponsor push — and write them down. Deciding this in month twelve means nobody argues about it in month two.

A three-person committee should still keep a decision log and a seat sheet. They take ten minutes a month, and they are the only reason a fourth person can join in month six without a two-hour briefing.

## The first meeting: an hour that assigns the seats

The first committee meeting is not a planning meeting. It is the meeting where the committee decides how it will decide, and it is worth one hour of everybody's time before any task is discussed.

| Minutes | What happens | Who leads |
|---|---|---|
| **0–5** | The purpose of the event in one sentence, read aloud. If two people disagree with the sentence, stop here — everything below is built on it. | Chair |
| **5–15** | The eight seats, read out from the table on this page, with the third column — what each seat decides alone — emphasized. | Chair |
| **15–30** | Names against seats. One name each. Anyone taking two seats says so out loud, and says which of the two they will do second. | Everyone |
| **30–40** | The spending table, in dollars. What each seat may commit alone, and what comes to the committee. | Finance |
| **40–50** | The first five rows of the decision table, argued and settled. Leave the rest for meeting two. | Chair |
| **50–60** | The date of the next meeting, the decision log opened, and one action per seat with a date on it. | Chair |

Two things not to do in this meeting. **Do not review the venue shortlist** — it will eat the hour and you will leave without names against seats, which is the whole point. And **do not let anyone leave without a job**, because a person who leaves a first meeting with no job does not come to the second one.

## Five failures a committee has that a checklist cannot catch

The calendar guides catch the missed task. These are the failures that come from the structure itself, and each one looks like normal behavior while it is happening.

**1. Two seats, one person, and nobody notices.** The most capable volunteer takes Chair, Program and Finance because they are the one who will actually do it. It works beautifully until the month that person has a hard time, and most of the event stops moving at once. The fix is not to take work away from the capable person. It is to give them the seat they are best at and find a second person for the seat that is merely administrative — and then let the second person be slower than they would have been.

**2. The seat that spends can also sign.** A venue lead who can sign a contract, or a program chair who can promise a fee, will eventually do it in a hurry, on a phone call, because the alternative was saying "let me check" to a vendor who was being nice. Separation is not a comment on anyone's judgment. It is what lets a volunteer say "I need to check" without it being personal.

**3. The committee decides everything, so it decides nothing.** When every decision goes to the full committee, the committee becomes a bottleneck and the real decisions move to the corridor before the meeting. The third column of the seat table exists to prevent this: name the decisions that do not need the room.

**4. Nobody can tell you what was promised.** A sponsor was told a logo on the program. A venue was told a speaker would arrive at nine. A partner was told they could have a table in the lobby. None of it is written anywhere, and all of it is now a commitment the event has to keep. The commitment register and the decision log are the two documents that stop this, and both take a minute per entry.

**5. The seat goes empty in month four.** A volunteer changes jobs, has a baby, moves, or simply stops answering. The work does not announce its absence — it just quietly does not happen, and you find out when the printer calls. The next section is about that.

## When a seat goes empty

Every committee loses a seat, and the ones that survive it are the ones that decided in advance what would happen. Four habits make the difference.

**Keep the seat sheet current, and keep it printed.** Titles printed, names in pen. When someone steps down you reprint one page, and the replacement inherits a described job rather than a pile of email.

**Every seat keeps its own one-page handover.** Not a diary — a page that says: what I own, what I decide alone, what is in flight right now, where the documents are, and the three things that will go wrong first if nobody picks this up. Ask for it at the start of the year, when it takes twenty minutes to write, rather than at the end, when it takes a resignation to prompt.

**The Chair names the empty seat out loud at the next meeting.** "Program chair is vacant; here is what is not happening until it is filled." A vacancy that is announced gets filled. A vacancy that is quietly absorbed becomes a second full-time job for whoever is most conscientious.

**Decide what gets paused, not just who takes over.** Some seats can be covered by a less experienced volunteer at reduced scope — the volunteer roster can run three weeks late without hurting anyone. Others cannot: the money seat and the contract seat cannot go dark. Write down, in advance, which two seats may not be left empty for a single week, so that a gap in those gets escalated immediately and a gap elsewhere does not panic anybody.

### What goes on a seat's handover page

One side of one sheet, written by the person holding the seat, kept with the seat sheet.

- [ ] **What I own**, in three lines, in my own words.
- [ ] **What I can decide alone**, and the amount, if there is one.
- [ ] **What is in flight right now**, with the date each thing is due.
- [ ] **Where the documents are** — the folder, the register, the login holder, the signed copies.
- [ ] **The three things that will go wrong first** if nobody picks this up, in the order they will happen.
- [ ] **Who I was about to call**, and what about.

## The seat sheet

Print this, write in names and phone numbers in pen, and give every seat a copy. It is the whole page in one place — the committee roles and responsibilities, the spending limits and the handover — on a single sheet you can pin up or fold into a folder.

| Seat | Name | Phone | Decides alone up to | Handover page written |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chair | | | $ | |
| Program chair | | | $ | |
| Venue and logistics lead | | | $ | |
| Finance and contracts lead | | | $ | |
| Registration and attendee care lead | | | $ | |
| Marketing and communications lead | | | $ | |
| Volunteer coordinator | | | $ | |
| Sponsorship and partnerships lead | | | $ | |

**The four questions this sheet should let anyone answer at any point in the year:**

- Who owns the money, and who else has looked at it this month?
- Who can sign, and what is the last thing they signed?
- If the person running the program disappeared tomorrow, what would stop?
- Which decisions are waiting for the next meeting, and what does waiting cost?

If any of the four takes more than a minute to answer, the structure is not finished yet — and it is much cheaper to finish it in month twelve than to explain it in month eleven.
