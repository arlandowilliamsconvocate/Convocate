# How much should I pay a guest speaker?

How to arrive at a number for one speaker without guessing: what drives a fee, what an honorarium is, what travel really costs, and what to put in writing once you have decided.

You have a name, a date and a room. What you do not have is a number, and the reason it feels hard is that nobody publishes one. Speaker bureaus quote fees for people whose faces are on billboards. The person you want is a professor, a pastor, a founder, a coach, or a colleague who is good on a stage — and there is no listing for that.

So this page does not give you a number. It gives you the reasoning that produces one, the questions that stop you guessing, and a worksheet to write the answer on. By the end you should be able to name a figure out loud and explain it to your committee in two sentences.

**This page is not legal or tax advice, and it is not a substitute for your accountant.** Where tax comes up, the section below tells you what to ask and who to ask.

**What this page does not do.** Three of our other guides already own the parts around this one, and none of them is repeated here. The [conference budget template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-budget-template) owns the budget line and the percentage arithmetic — this page tells you what belongs *in* that line, not how to divide your total. The [speaker invitation letter template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-invitation-letter) owns the wording of the ask, including the email that raises the fee question politely. The [speaker contract template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-contract-template) owns the clause language once you have agreed a number. This page is the decision in the middle: what to pay, and why.

**How to use it.** If you are in a hurry, read "The short answer", then jump to "What to ask the speaker or their office" and send the questions today. Everything else is for the hour when you sit down with the budget.

## The short answer

There is no correct fee for a guest speaker, because a fee is not a price for a product — it is an agreement about what one person is doing for your event, how far they are traveling to do it, and what else they are giving up to be there.

What you can do is decide deliberately instead of guessing. That means four moves:

1. **Find out whether they have a number already.** Most people who speak regularly either have a fee schedule or know what they were paid last time. Ask before you offer, because a number you name first is the number you are negotiating from.
2. **Decide what your event can carry**, using the budget line rather than a feeling.
3. **Price the travel separately**, so that "we will cover your expenses" never gets confused with the fee itself.
4. **Write the number down** with what it includes, before anyone books a flight.

Everything below is detail on those four moves.

One thing worth saying plainly at the start: the fear on this page is usually not that you will pay too much. It is that you will offer something insulting, or that you will be taken advantage of, and you will not know which has happened until it is too late to fix it. The way out of both is the same — ask early, name a number, and put it in writing.

## Why you will not find a published average here

You have probably already searched for one. You will find pages that publish ranges, and if you compare three of them you will find numbers that do not agree with each other by a factor of ten.

Here is why, and why we are not going to add to the pile.

**The published ranges describe a different market.** The sites that publish fee ranges are, in the main, speaker bureaus and agencies. Their business is booking people who command a fee that is a marketing event in itself — the retired athlete, the television name, the bestseller on a tour. Those numbers are real for those speakers. They tell you nothing about what to pay a regional speaker for a 200-seat conference, and a page that quotes them as if they were general is quietly answering a question you did not ask.

**A fee is set by the engagement, not by the person's category.** The same speaker may charge one number for a 45-minute keynote in their own city and a very different one for two days, three sessions, a workshop and a flight. Any "average" blends those into a figure that describes nobody's actual booking.

**Nobody measures this.** There is no survey we know of that collects what real conferences of 100 to 500 seats paid their speakers, and if there were, we would still be cautious: the honest answer would be a wide spread with a small sample at each end. We have no customers, no dataset and no way to check a figure, so we are not going to invent one and dress it as research. A made-up number on a money page is worse than no number, because you might budget against it.

**What we will give you instead** is the reasoning, the questions and a rule of thumb that is labeled as a rule of thumb — ours, not research — in the budget guide this page links to. And one genuinely published figure you can check yourself, for the travel half of the cost, which is the [GSA per diem rates](https://www.gsa.gov/travel/plan-book/per-diem-rates) published by the U.S. General Services Administration. Those are the rates the federal government uses to reimburse its own travelers: a lodging rate and a meals rate for a specific city and month, updated as the government updates them. They are not a speaker fee and they are not a market rate, but if you need a defensible starting point for "what does a night and a day of food cost in this city in April", that is a real, checkable number and it is published by someone who is not selling you anything.

## What a fee is, and what an honorarium is

The two words get used interchangeably, and they are not the same thing. Getting the word right changes the conversation, so it is worth thirty seconds.

**A fee** is a price for a service. It is quoted, it is negotiated, it is invoiced, and it is expected to reflect the market for the speaker's time. Someone who speaks for a living quotes fees. If you are booking a professional speaker, this is the frame, and it is not rude to treat it as a business transaction — they will.

**An honorarium** is a gift of thanks for a service for which no fee was set. It is the word used by universities, associations, nonprofits, hospitals and school districts — anywhere a guest gives their expertise and the organization wants to recognize it without pretending to buy it. An honorarium is usually smaller than a commercial fee and it is usually not negotiated. It says: we know this was worth something, and this is our thanks.

**What that means in practice:**

- If the speaker has a fee schedule, you are in a fee conversation. Ask what it is.
- If the speaker says "no fee" or "whatever you think is right", you are in an honorarium conversation, and the worst thing you can do is take them at their word and offer nothing. A "no fee" speaker is rarely free — travel, a night away, meals and a gift are still costs, and the budget guide says the same thing about the line.
- If you are the one who wants to frame it as an honorarium, say so out loud. "We would like to give you an honorarium rather than a fee — is that all right?" is a sentence that prevents a misunderstanding six weeks later.
- **The word does not change what it costs you, and it does not change what the recipient owes in tax.** It changes the tone. Do not let it change the arithmetic.

There is one more distinction people miss: **the gift is not the fee.** A plaque, a basket, a dinner and a gift card are thank-yous, not payment, and a speaker who was expecting a fee will not be made whole by a gift. Budget them as a separate small line if you want to give them, and never present a gift as the honorarium unless you have said clearly that it is.

## What actually drives the number

When a speaker quotes a figure, it is the sum of the things in this table. When you wonder whether a number is reasonable, this is the list to run down — it will usually explain the difference between two quotes that seemed impossible to compare.

| What it is | The question to ask | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| **The role in the program** | Is this the keynote, a workshop, a panel seat, or the host for the day? | A keynote is the most visible slot and usually the most expensive. A panel seat is often a fraction. Hosting a whole day is a different job and sometimes a bigger one. |
| **How many sessions, and how much time on site** | One talk, or three? Are they here for an hour, a morning, or two days? | Time on site is the real unit. Two sessions are usually two fees, or a discount on the second — but never assume the second is free. Ask. |
| **Distance and nights away** | Where are they coming from, and how many nights does that add? | A local speaker and a cross-country speaker at the same fee are not the same cost to you. Travel is budgeted separately, and the budget guide gives it its own line for exactly this reason. |
| **What they are giving up** | Is this a working day for them? Are they turning down another booking? | A speaker who loses a paid day to travel to you has a real cost. It is fair for that to appear in the number. |
| **Preparation** | Is the talk new for you, or one they have given before? | A bespoke talk for your audience is hours of unpaid work. A talk they have refined for years costs them an evening. It is reasonable for that to be visible in the fee. |
| **Recording, streaming and rights** | May we record it, stream it, post clips, sell it, or keep it in a library? | This is the line people forget, and it is often the one the speaker cares about most. The more rights you want, the more the fee tends to be — or the more likely they say no. Decide what you actually need before you ask. |
| **Products from the stage** | Will they sell or promote books or programs, and is that part of their income? | For some speakers the fee is secondary to what the back of the room earns. That can make a fee negotiable — and it is a fair thing to raise directly. |
| **Reputation and how booked they are** | Is this person in demand this year? Do they have an agent, a publisher's tour, or an employer who controls their calendar? | Demand is demand. An agent or a publisher will also change who you are negotiating with and how long it takes. |
| **Who else is paying them** | Is their employer, university, denomination or organization covering part of this? | Sometimes the trip is already funded and the fee is smaller than you feared. It costs one question to find out. |
| **What you are actually buying** | Is their name going to bring people who would not otherwise come? | If yes, that is part of the value and it is fair to pay for it. If you are not sure, be honest with yourself before you let it inflate the number. |

A number is not automatically too high because it is bigger than you hoped, and it is not automatically fair because it matches last year. Run the list, and you will usually find the reason.

## Five ways to arrive at a number

Use more than one of these. Where they agree, you have your answer. Where they disagree, the gap is the conversation you need to have.

### 1. Ask what they charge

The most obvious move and the one people skip, because asking feels like admitting you cannot afford them. Ask anyway, early, in writing, and without apology. Most people who speak regularly will simply tell you. The invitation letter guide has the wording for the email that does it without awkwardness.

If the answer is "I don't have a fee, whatever you think is appropriate", you have learned something useful: the number is now yours to name, and naming a low one is not humility — it is a decision you made on their behalf.

### 2. Ask what they were paid for a comparable event

This is the question people are most afraid of, and it is the most informative. Not "what is your rate" but "the last time you did something like this — a one-day regional conference, a few hundred people — what did that look like?" Many speakers will answer. Some will not, and that is fine; you have lost nothing.

Do not ask this of a speaker you have just met at a networking event, and do not ask it in a group email. Ask the person who is arranging the booking, once, directly.

### 3. Work backwards from what your event can carry

Take your total budget and look at the share that belongs to speaker fees. Our [conference budget template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-budget-template) puts the speaker fee line at **8 to 15 percent of the total**, with travel as its own line at 4 to 8 percent. That range is a **rule of thumb we wrote, not a survey** — it is there to tell you when a line looks out of proportion, not to set your fee. Treat it the way you would treat a friend's opinion: useful, checkable, not authoritative.

Two things follow from it, and they matter more than the range itself:

- **The fee is a fixed cost.** It does not shrink when attendance is lower. On a 100-seat event, the same fee is a much bigger share of a smaller budget, which is why small events often sit at the top of any range and above it.
- **The check is a prompt, not a verdict.** A fee far above the range is worth a second look; a fee far below it usually means somebody is being underpaid, or that travel is quietly hidden somewhere else.

If the arithmetic says you can carry a number, that is your ceiling. If the speaker's number is above it, you have three honest options: find the money elsewhere, ask whether a shorter or single-session version is available, or say no and book someone else. All three are better than agreeing to a number you cannot pay in the month it is due.

### 4. Work backwards from what the speaker brings

For a conference with paid registration, it is reasonable to ask what a named speaker is worth in registrations. Be careful here, because the honest answer is that **you probably cannot measure it.** Registrations arrive from many directions, and unless you ran two identical events you cannot isolate one speaker's effect.

So use this as judgement, not arithmetic: is this the name that makes the flyer work? Will a sponsor or a partner say yes because of them? If the answer is a clear yes, that is worth paying for. If the answer is "it would be nice", you are buying a preference, and preferences have a smaller budget.

### 5. Name a number you can defend, and say it out loud

Eventually you have to say a figure. Do it in one sentence, with a range if you can afford the top of it:

> "We can offer $X as a fee, plus travel and one night's lodging. If you are able to do the second session as well, we can go to $Y."

That sentence does four things: it names a number, it separates travel from the fee, it leaves room to negotiate upward for more work rather than downward for less, and it is short enough to say on a phone call without losing your nerve.

If the answer is no, ask what number would work. It is not a rejection of you; it is information, and it is much cheaper to get now than in the week the invoice arrives.

## What to ask the speaker or their office

Send these in one email, as questions rather than as a form. The three that matter most are the first three; the rest decide how the number behaves later.

**The three core questions:**

1. **"What do you charge for an event like ours?"** Include the shape of the event in the same email: date, audience size, one session or more, how long, and where. A quote without that information is a guess in both directions.
2. **"What does that include?"** One session or the whole day? A preparation call? Materials? The panel or the Q&A after? A book signing? The difference between two quotes is very often this answer, not the number.
3. **"Is travel separate, and how do you handle it?"** Booked by you or by them, reimbursed at cost or at a flat rate, and is there a cap. Ask before you agree to anything, because this is the line that grows after the contract is signed.

**Then, before you settle:**

4. **"Who else needs to agree to this?"** An agent, a publisher, an employer, a board, a spouse. If you are negotiating with someone who has to check with someone else, everything takes a week longer, and your deposit date does not move.
5. **"Are you already going to be in the area around those dates?"** If they are, the travel may shrink or disappear, and it is reasonable to ask whether that changes the number.
6. **"Do you have a fee for a nonprofit, a school or a smaller organization?"** Ask it as a question, not an assumption. Some speakers have a lower rate and will offer it. Others do not, and asking has cost you nothing.
7. **"If we want to record it and put it online, does that change the fee?"** Ask before you promise your committee a video library. The answer is sometimes "no, but send me the file", and sometimes a second fee.
8. **"How and when would you like to be paid?"** One payment or a deposit and a balance? Invoice or receipt? Paid to you personally or to your organization? That last one matters for the accountant section below.
9. **"Is there anything you need from us to say yes?"** Sometimes it is a green room, sometimes it is a projector, sometimes it is knowing the session length. Cheap to ask, awkward to discover in the last week.

**One rule on all of these:** get the answer in writing, even if it is a reply to your email saying "yes, that's right". A number agreed on a phone call is a number two people remember differently.

## Travel, lodging and the things that are not the fee

This is where most speaker budgets quietly break, because travel is the part nobody negotiates and everybody underestimates.

**"We will cover your expenses" is not a fee.** It is a promise to reimburse, and it is not a number the speaker can plan around. If you mean to include travel in a single payment, say so explicitly: "The fee is $X and it includes travel." Otherwise the two are separate and should stay separate in the budget.

**What the travel line actually contains:**

- **Getting there.** Flight or rail, and the ground transport at both ends — airport to hotel, hotel to venue, and back. Rideshare and parking are small and they add up.
- **The night before.** For a morning keynote, a speaker arriving the same morning is one delayed flight away from an empty stage. The night before is not a luxury; it is insurance. Budget it unless the speaker is local.
- **The night after, if the program ends late.** Asking someone to speak at 8 p.m. and then catch a flight is asking them to leave early or to arrive home at 2 a.m.
- **Meals while traveling**, and sometimes a per diem. If you are not sure what a day of food costs in that city, the [GSA per diem rates](https://www.gsa.gov/travel/plan-book/per-diem-rates) give a published figure by city and month. It is a government reimbursement rate, not a market rate, but it is checkable and it is not a guess.
- **Baggage, seat selection, a second bag for materials, and the change fee when the date moves.** These are the lines that appear after the fact and get argued about.
- **Local speakers:** agree a figure for mileage before the trip rather than after. A per-mile rate agreed in advance is a receipt; a reimbursement request in December is a conversation.

**Three decisions to make before anyone books anything:**

1. **Who books.** If you book, you control the fare and you own the change fees. If the speaker books and invoices you, you may be paying a marked-up fare, and you will not know. Pick one, write it down, and be consistent.
2. **What the cap is.** Agree a maximum you will reimburse for travel and lodging, in writing. Without a cap, the request for a more expensive seat arrives eventually, and refusing it then is worse than setting the limit now.
3. **What "reasonable" means.** Coach, standard room, one night. Say it in the same sentence as the number. Most speakers will not argue with it; the ones who do are telling you something useful.

**The cost people forget entirely:** the speaker's time on site that is not on stage. A green room for two hours, a dinner the night before, an hour of photographs. None of it is billed, all of it is why some speakers ask for a bigger number, and none of it appears in a budget unless you decide it should.

## Are speaker honorariums taxable?

Yes, almost certainly — a payment for speaking is income to the person who receives it, whether you call it a fee, an honorarium, a gift or a love offering. The label you use does not decide the tax treatment; the nature of the payment does.

That is the limit of what we can say, and it is why the rest of this section is questions rather than answers. **Take these to your accountant, or to whoever handles the organization's books, before you pay anyone.** It is a twenty-minute conversation that prevents a January problem.

**What the IRS publishes, and what it does not.** The IRS instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC (at irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec) tell you *when to file an information return*, which is a different question from what the speaker owes. Two statements in those instructions are worth knowing before you call your accountant:

- The instructions say to file Form 1099-NEC for payments of **at least $2,000** in a year for services performed by someone who is not your employee. Check the current version before you rely on the figure: this threshold has moved in recent years, and a lot of material online still quotes an older number.
- The instructions also say that reporting is required for payments made in the course of a trade or business, that "nonprofit organizations are considered to be engaged in a trade or business and are subject to these reporting requirements", and that certain organizations exempt under section 501(c) or (d) are among those subject. Read the current text yourself if this matters to you, and let your accountant tell you which category your organization is in.

Neither of those statements tells you what to do. They tell you what to ask about.

**The questions to put to your accountant:**

1. Is our payment to this speaker reportable on a Form 1099-NEC, and does the answer change because we are a nonprofit, a school, or a business?
2. Does calling it an honorarium rather than a fee change anything for tax purposes, or is the label irrelevant?
3. Do we need a W-9 from the speaker before we pay, and who should collect it?
4. If we reimburse travel separately, do we need receipts, and does the reimbursement count as income to the speaker or not?
5. If we pay the speaker's organization or company rather than the speaker personally, does that change what we file?
6. Is there any risk we are treating this person as an employee rather than an independent contractor, given that we set the date, the room and the schedule?
7. If we give a gift instead of cash, is that reportable?
8. What do we file, by when, and what happens if we miss the deadline?
9. Does our state treat any of this differently from the federal rules?
10. What records should we keep, and for how long?

**What the speaker will expect from you.** A speaker who is paid regularly will often ask for a W-9 to be sent to them, or ask who the payment comes from. Answer plainly and do not improvise: "our bookkeeper handles that, I will confirm this week" is a perfectly good answer, and much better than a confident answer that turns out to be wrong.

**What not to promise.** Do not tell a speaker their honorarium is tax-free, and do not tell them it is taxable. You are not their accountant, and if you are wrong in either direction it becomes their problem and your reputation.

## What a church or small nonprofit typically does about a guest speaker's fee

*This is the one section of this page written for churches and small ministries. Everything else on the page applies to any organizer.*

A church booking a guest speaker is usually in a different conversation from a conference booking a keynote, and it is worth naming why: the money is often tighter, the relationship is usually personal, and the vocabulary is different. What follows is how churches and small nonprofits generally handle it — not a rule, and not a number, because there is no published figure for this and anyone who quotes one is describing their own region and their own experience.

**The word is honorarium, and it changes the tone.** In a church setting, a guest preacher or teacher is usually not quoting a commercial fee; they are being thanked for ministry. Framing it as an honorarium says "we want to recognize this" rather than "we are buying your hour", and it is the normal word in that world. It does not make the payment optional, and it does not make it smaller by definition — it sets the register of the conversation.

**Two mechanisms, and churches use both.**

- **A set honorarium.** A figure decided in advance, agreed with the speaker, and paid whether the offering was large or small. It is the clearest thing to budget and the easiest thing for the speaker to plan around. If you can name a number, name it.
- **A love offering.** A collection taken for the guest, either as the whole gift or on top of a set amount. It has the virtue of being generous when the congregation is generous, and the flaw of being unpredictable: nobody can plan around it, and a small offering on a low-attendance Sunday is an awkward thing to hand someone. If you use one, say so in advance, and consider guaranteeing a minimum so the speaker is not carrying the risk of your attendance. If you take a love offering *in addition to* a set amount, say that too — "there will be a love offering as well" is a welcome sentence, and being surprised by one is not.

**Travel is separate here too.** Churches often cover travel by a combination of mileage, a night in a member's home, and meals — which is genuinely hospitable and worth offering. Two cautions. First, put the arrangement in writing even when it is friendly, because "we will put you up" becomes "we assumed you had a hotel" more often than anyone expects. Second, if the speaker is staying in a home, ask them first: some welcome it, some need a quiet room and a door that locks, and nobody will say so unless you ask. Mileage, flights and a hotel night should be named as separate from the honorarium, the same way they are for any other event.

**What to put in writing.** The same five things as any other booking, in the plainest language you can manage:

1. The amount, or the mechanism if it is a love offering, plus any guaranteed minimum.
2. Whether travel, lodging and meals are covered on top, and who arranges what.
3. When it is paid — before, on the day, or in the week after — and by what method.
4. Who it is payable to: the speaker personally, or their ministry or church. This one has tax consequences, which is a question for your treasurer or accountant, not for this page.
5. What the honorarium covers: one service, or the morning and the evening; one session or the whole weekend.

**The things churches get wrong most often.** Assuming a visiting minister will not accept anything, and offering nothing — which is both unkind and, in most traditions, not what the guest expected. Assuming a friend is free, when the friend has taken a day off and driven three hours. Treating the love offering as a substitute for a decision, so that nobody ever names a number. And the reverse: assuming the speaker wants a large honorarium when what they actually need is a covered flight and a clear schedule.

**And the honest bit.** If you are a small church with a small budget, say so. "We can offer $X and cover your travel, and we know that is modest" is a sentence most guest speakers have heard before and will answer honestly. What people find difficult is not a small number; it is being asked to guess what you can afford.

## Putting the number in writing

Once you have a figure, write it down within the week, while both of you remember the same conversation. The [speaker contract template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-contract-template) has the clause language — its fee, payment and travel sections are the three that carry this — so this page will not repeat it. What belongs here is the short list of what the clause has to say, so you notice if one is missing:

- **The amount**, and what it includes.
- **The sessions it covers**, and what a second one costs.
- **When it is paid**, and whether any of it is a deposit.
- **The travel arrangement**: who books, what the cap is, what is reimbursed and on what receipts.
- **The recording and streaming rights**, in whatever words you agreed.
- **What happens if the date moves or either side cancels.**

If you are still negotiating rather than signing, the [venue and speaker contract checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) has the eight questions to settle before a speaker contract is signed, and it is the right page to read before this one becomes a document.

When it is signed, the number stops being a worry and becomes a line in the budget with a date attached — which is the whole point.

## What this page does not answer

**Whether this speaker is worth it.** That is a judgement about your audience, your program and your money, and nobody outside your committee can make it.

**What other organizers actually pay.** We have no survey, no customers and no dataset, and we are not going to publish a number we cannot stand behind. If you want a real comparison, ask two or three people who ran a similar event in the last year. They will tell you more than any page will.

**Whether the honorarium is tax-deductible for you, or reportable by them.** Your accountant, every time. The questions above are the ones to take with you.

**What to do about a speaker who wants a fee you cannot pay.** The honest answer is in "Five ways to arrive at a number": say no, ask what would work, or find someone else. There is no clever third path, and agreeing to a number you cannot pay is the one mistake that costs you the event.
