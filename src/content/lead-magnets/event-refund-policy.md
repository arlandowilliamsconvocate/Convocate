# Event cancellation policy template

The question arrives by email, usually at an awkward hour, and it is short: *we can't come after all — can we get our money back?* Whatever you answer in that moment becomes your policy, whether or not you ever wrote one down. A written policy does not change the answer. It changes who decided it, and when.

This page is an event cancellation policy template for that decision, in both directions. It sets out how to work out what you can actually pay back and when, how to write the attendee-facing policy in plain words, and how to read the venue-facing cancellation clause so that the two do not contradict each other. It is written for an organizer with registration open, or about to open, who would rather settle this now than inside a thread.

The order matters, and it is the opposite of what most templates do. Most of them start with the wording. Start with the money. The amount you can honestly offer an attendee is set by what you have already spent and what you can still get back, and no sentence is worth writing until you have done that arithmetic. Once you have, the sentence usually writes itself, because there is only one true thing to say.

**What this page does not do.** The [vendor contract checklist](/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) owns the questions to put to a venue or a speaker about cancellation *before you sign* — the clause, the ladder, the events that excuse performance. This page owns the other side of the same money: what you tell the person who bought a ticket, and whether the two promises can both be kept. If a contract is still unsigned, read the checklist first and come back. If registration is already open, you are in the right place, and the first section is the one to read twice.

---

## The two policies, and why they have to fit together

Every event has two refund policies, written by two different people, about the same money.

The first is a **contract clause**. It says what happens to the deposit you paid the venue if you cancel, and what the venue owes you if it cancels on you. You did not write it. It was written for the venue's benefit, by someone whose job is to protect the venue, and it stops being negotiable the moment you sign.

The second is your **attendee policy**. It says what happens to the registration fee if the attendee cannot come, or if you cannot run the event. You write this one, and it is the one a person reads at the moment they are deciding whether to trust you with their money.

The two are about the same pile of cash, and this is the mistake nearly every template makes: it writes the attendee policy as though the money is all still sitting there. It is not. By the time registration opens, part of it is a deposit that has already left your account, part of it is committed to a caterer on a count you cannot lower for free, and part of it is a speaker who has cleared a date in their calendar. The attendee is asking for a refund out of money you may no longer hold.

So there is one rule, and everything else on this page follows from it:

**Your attendee policy may promise less than your contract lets you recover. It must never promise more.**

The gap between the two is your margin of safety. It is also the reason to write both in the same week. If the person who negotiates the venue contract and the person who writes the registration page are not in the same conversation, the gap is not a decision — it is an accident, and it surfaces the first time someone cancels.

The rest of this page is that arithmetic, in the order you would do it: what comes back from the venue, what is already spent and cannot come back, where your own deadline has to sit so that the money is still there when someone asks, and what to say — and what not to write — when they do.

## What you can actually pay back

The question is never "how much did we take?" It is "how much do we still hold, and how much of that is already promised to someone else?"

Money for an event sits in three states, and only the third one is refundable.

| State | What it looks like | Can it go back to an attendee? |
|---|---|---|
| **Spent and unrecoverable** | A venue deposit paid at signing, print and signage, insurance, a speaker deposit, a deposit on equipment | No. This money is gone, and a refund from it is a gift from your own funds |
| **Committed but not yet paid** | A venue balance due on a date, a catering count that can still be lowered, a speaker balance due after the event | Only if you act before the date. This is the part a calendar can still save |
| **Held and uncommitted** | Registration fees received, sponsorship money not yet spent, donations | Yes. This is the refund pool, and it is smaller than the bank balance suggests |

**The refund pool is not the bank balance.** This is the single most useful sentence on the page. An event can look healthy and be unable to refund anybody, because the deposit left in March, the registrations arrived in September, and the money that is left is earmarked for a balance due in October. Cash flow and profit are different things, and a refund is a cash flow event.

Write the pool down before you write the policy:

1. **Registrations received so far:** the amount actually in the account, not the amount you expect.
2. **Minus every payment still due before the event:** the venue balance, the speaker balance, catering, AV, print.
3. **Minus the reserve you are not willing to touch** — the money that keeps the event solvent if attendance lands where it might rather than where you hoped.
4. **What is left is the honest refund pool.**

If that number is smaller than the policy you want to publish, you have three honest options and no fourth. You can promise less. You can price so that the pool is bigger. Or you can reduce the exposure — a smaller deposit, a later payment date, a contract that returns more if you cancel early. All three are decisions; the fourth option, promising anyway, is not a decision, it is a delay.

### The date that matters more than the amount

Most refund arguments are not about money. They are about timing.

An attendee who cancels six weeks out and an attendee who cancels two days out are in completely different positions, and it is not unfair to treat them differently — it is accurate. Six weeks out, their place can still be sold and your costs can still be adjusted. Two days out, the catering count is final, the name badge is printed, and the seat is empty. A policy that offers the same answer to both is either over-generous early or cold late, and in practice it becomes the second one.

This is why the policy should be a **ladder of dates**, not a sentence about goodwill. A ladder has three properties worth designing deliberately:

- **Each rung is a date, printed as a date.** "Until 5:00 pm on 12 September", not "up to 30 days before". Day counts make the reader do arithmetic, and the arithmetic is where the argument starts.
- **Each rung matches something real.** The rung should sit on or just before a date when your own costs change — a balance due, a count to confirm, a printing deadline. A rung that corresponds to nothing is an arbitrary cut, and an arbitrary cut is the one people push against.
- **The ladder is the same on every page it appears.** The registration page, the confirmation email, the ticket and the answer you send must agree. Two versions of a refund deadline is not a mistake an attendee forgives, because it looks like a trap even when it is only sloppiness.

### The reserve, in one paragraph

Set aside, at the start, the amount you would need to refund everyone who is likely to ask. It does not have to be the whole pool. It has to be enough that the first three requests are paid the same day, without a conversation about whether the event can afford it. The moment a refund depends on next month's registrations, the policy has quietly become "we will see", and everyone who asks can hear it. If you have not built the cost side of the event yet, the [budget template](/resources/conference-budget-template) is where the deposit, the catering count and the print line live, and the reserve belongs on it as a line of its own. A reserve is not pessimism. It is the price of being able to keep a promise.

## The three questions to answer before you write a word

Every refund policy, however long, answers three questions. If you can answer them in order, the wording is a formatting exercise.

**One: what is refundable?** The whole fee, part of it, or none of it — and if part, then what the retained amount is *for*. "A $__ handling fee" is a defensible answer if it is stated in dollars and appears before payment. "A small fee" is not an answer; it is a promise to negotiate later, with someone who is already annoyed.

**Two: on what clock?** The date, the hour, and the channel. A deadline of "before the event" means nothing, because everyone believes they are before the event until they are not. Write the date. Write the time. Say where the request has to arrive — the address, not "let us know".

**Three: in what form does the money come back?** Back to the card that paid, as a credit against next year, as a transfer to another person, or not at all. These are not equivalent to the attendee and they are not equivalent to your budget, and choosing deliberately is the difference between a policy that solves the problem and one that defers it.

There is a fourth question, and it is the one that decides whether the other three survive contact with reality: **who decides the exceptions, and how fast do they answer?** Name a person, not a committee. Give them a deadline — one business day is the right shape, because goodwill decays faster than information arrives. A named person with a day to answer will make a slightly generous call and keep the relationship. A committee with a week to answer will make a cautious call and lose the attendee either way.

## The four things an attendee can be offered

Before you write a policy, notice that "refund" is only one of four answers, and it is not usually the one that helps them most.

| Option | What it costs you | When it is the right answer | The trap in the wording |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Money back to the card** | Cash out of the refund pool, at the worst possible moment | The money is still held and the date is early enough | "Full refund" with no date attached reads as a standing promise |
| **Transfer to another person** | Nothing. The seat stays filled and the fee stays earned | The attendee's problem is a clash, not a change of heart | Calling it a favor instead of a right, or leaving out the deadline for the name change |
| **Credit toward the next event** | Nothing today, and a liability on next year's budget | The event repeats, and the attendee intends to come | "Credit" with no expiry, no transfer rule and no amount written down |
| **Nothing** | Nothing, and it costs you the relationship if it is a surprise | The fee was small, the cost was already spent, and this was stated plainly before payment | "Non-refundable" discovered at the door is the version people dispute |

Two things follow from that table.

**Offer the transfer first, in the policy itself.** A transfer costs you nothing, keeps the seat filled, and solves the most common reason a person cannot come — something else is happening that day. If it only appears in the email, the attendee discovers it after working up the nerve to ask. Put it in the policy as a right, with a deadline for the name change, and a share of your refund requests never become requests at all.

**Write the credit down properly or do not offer it.** A credit is a promise to deliver something next year, and if next year's event does not happen, the credit is a debt. Give it an expiry, say whether it can be transferred, say what it is worth, and record who holds one. An untracked credit is how an event acquires a second, invisible refund liability that nobody budgeted for.

And the fourth option deserves a defense, because it is the one people write badly. It is legitimate to say that a fee is not refundable — if the money was spent on the person's behalf, if the price barely covered the cost, if the event is a fundraiser where the fee is a contribution. What makes it legitimate is that it was visible **before** the payment, in the same size type as the price. Said at the door, it is not a policy. It is a surprise, and surprises are what card disputes are made of.

## The cancellation ladder

Here is the ladder as a framework, with the amounts and dates left blank for you to fill in. The point is not the numbers — they are yours — but that every rung is a decision you have already made rather than one you will make under pressure.

| When they cancel | What you still hold | What the policy should say | Why that rung sits there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Before [date 1]** | Everything, minus nothing | Full refund, or a transfer, on request | No cost has been committed on their behalf yet |
| **Between [date 1] and [date 2]** | Most of it, minus the deposit you cannot recover | Refund less the amount already spent on their place, stated in dollars | This is the window where your own exposure starts |
| **Between [date 2] and [date 3]** | Little, because the count is confirmed and the print is ordered | Transfer to another person, or a credit; refund at your discretion, in writing | The seat can still be filled, so offer the thing that fills it |
| **Between [date 3] and the day** | Nothing | No refund; a transfer if a name can still be changed | The costs are final and the badge is printed |
| **Day of the event, or a no-show** | Nothing | No refund, and say so kindly | The person's place was held and paid for |
| **If you cancel** | Whatever the venue and other contracts return | Full refund of the fee, on a stated date | You broke the promise, so the attendee should not carry the cost |
| **If you postpone** | Everything, because nothing is lost yet | The fee carries to the new date, with a refund on request | A postponement is not a cancellation, and attendees should be able to leave |

Fill the ladder in this order: **start with the rows where the cancellation is yours.** Decide what happens if *you* cancel, and if you postpone, before you decide what happens if they do. That order is not sentiment; it is arithmetic. Those rows are the ones funded from your own money, and they are the rows that tell you how much reserve the others need to keep.

Then set the dates so that the ladder descends when your costs step up. If your venue balance is due on a date, the rung above it should be the last one that offers a full refund. If the caterer wants a final count on a date, that date is a natural cut. Your ladder should look like your payment schedule, because it is the same schedule.

Two details that decide whether the ladder holds:

**State the retained amount in dollars, at the rung.** "Refund less the deposit" invites a discussion about what the deposit was. "Refund less $__" ends it. The number is yours to choose; the clarity is not optional.

**Say what happens to the money in the middle.** When a refund is partial, say when the partial amount is paid — the same week, not "after the event". A partial refund paid late is experienced as a full refund denied.

## Who is cancelling, and what counts as cancelled

A policy that only describes one kind of cancellation is half a policy. There are seven situations, and a reader can tell within a paragraph whether you thought about the ones that are not their fault.

### The attendee cancels

The ordinary case, and the one the ladder above handles. Two things make it go well. The deadline is a date they can find without asking. And the first answer offered is the transfer, not the refund, because a transfer solves the problem they actually have.

### The attendee does not come and does not tell you

A no-show is a cancellation that arrives late and silently. The policy should say what happens, and the honest answer is that nothing is refunded — the place was held and the cost was incurred. Say it once, in the policy, without moralizing. The people who read it and cancel properly are the ones you are protecting.

### You cancel

This is the row that decides whether your event deserves its reputation. If you cancel, the attendee did nothing wrong, and the policy should be unambiguous: the fee is returned in full, on a date you name, without the attendee having to ask twice or accept a credit they did not choose.

Name the date. "We will refund everyone within [number] business days of the announcement" is a policy. "Refunds will be processed" is a hope with a verb in front of it.

And be honest with yourself about the arithmetic before you publish it, because this is the promise funded from the gap between your refund pool and your costs. If the pool cannot cover a full refund of every fee, the venue contract was too heavy for the event, and that is worth discovering now rather than in the week you have to cancel.

### You postpone, or move the event

A postponement is not a cancellation, and attendees should not be treated as though it is. The default that works: the fee carries to the new date, and anyone who cannot make the new date gets a full refund on request, within a stated window. That gives the event a chance to survive and gives the attendee a real choice.

Say what happens if the new date is more than a certain distance away, because "postponed to a date to be confirmed" is not a date, and an attendee holding a ticket to an unnamed day has every right to ask for their money back.

### The event is shortened, moved, or changed

The second day lost to weather. The venue that floods and the event that moves to a smaller room. The conference that becomes a single-track day. None of these is a cancellation, and all of them cost the attendee something. Decide in advance whether a shortened event triggers a partial refund, and if it does not, say so — an attendee who discovers after the fact that the policy had no answer will construct one, usually with their card issuer.

The safest wording here is a commitment to a decision rather than a number: *if we shorten the event materially, we will tell you within [number] days and offer [a partial refund / a credit] — and we will not leave you to work out which.* It is honest, it is answerable, and it does not require you to price a flood in advance.

### The venue cancels, or the building is unavailable

You will be dealing with the venue under a contract, and the [vendor contract checklist](/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) is where the questions about that clause belong. From the attendee's side the answer is simpler than the contract: if the event cannot happen because the room is gone, the attendee gets their fee back, and the question of who ultimately funds it is between you and the venue. An attendee should never be told that a third party is holding their money. They paid you.

### Something outside anyone's control

Weather, travel disruption, illness, a public order that closes the building. Whatever your contract says about who carries that risk, the attendee-facing answer has to be written down, because this is the case where a vague policy becomes an argument in public.

Two workable shapes. Either the fee is refunded in full, or the fee is carried to a rescheduled date with a refund on request for anyone who cannot come. Both are defensible. What is not defensible is a policy that is generous in the abstract and silent here, because this is the situation where people will read it most closely and quote it back to you.

One more thing, and it is the sentence most policies miss: **say whether "cancelled" includes "postponed with no new date".** An event that is indefinitely postponed is a cancellation with softer language, and attendees know it.

## The venue side of the same promise

The checklist gets the clause in writing. What you do with the answer belongs here, because the clause is not only a legal term — it is the number that limits every sentence you are about to publish.

**The clause is your ceiling.** Whatever the venue returns to you if you cancel is the most you could hand to attendees, less everything else already spent. Do the subtraction before the wording. If the answer is uncomfortable, that is information about the contract, not about the policy.

**The clause is a calendar.** Its stages are dates on which your exposure changes. Those dates are the natural rungs of the attendee ladder, one step earlier. The practical rule: your last full-refund date should fall before the venue's first non-refundable step, so that the money an attendee is asking for has not already been committed elsewhere. If you get this backwards, late cancellations are funded from the reserve, and the reserve runs out before the event does.

**The clause is negotiable exactly once.** If the ladder the venue offers is worse than the policy you want to publish, one of the two has to move, and the only moment to move the contract is before signature. That conversation is short and unglamorous: *if we cancel this far out, what comes back?* Ask it while the venue still wants the booking. After signature, the answer is the contract.

**The speaker is the second ceiling.** A booked speaker is a person with a calendar and, usually, a fee with a schedule attached. If a cancellation close to the date means you owe the full fee, that amount comes out of the same pool as the attendee refunds. The [speaker contract template](/resources/speaker-contract-template) is where the language for that arrangement lives; the number to notice is what you owe if you cancel, because it is money that cannot go back to an attendee.

**And the other direction matters just as much.** What the venue owes *you* if it cancels is the fund that pays the attendee refunds in the case where you did nothing wrong. If that clause returns nothing, then in that scenario the refunds come from your reserve or from your own account, and the policy should be written by someone who knows it.

### When the two cannot be made to fit

Sometimes they cannot. The venue will not return the deposit after a certain date, and you want to offer full refunds until two weeks out. You have four moves, and they are worth naming so that you recognize which one you are making:

1. **Move the attendee deadline earlier**, to before the venue's step. Costs you some goodwill with late deciders; costs you no money.
2. **Keep the deadline and fund the gap** from the reserve, deliberately, with a number attached. This is a choice, not a leak, as long as the number is written down.
3. **Reduce the deposit** so that the gap is smaller. A smaller deposit is often available for a smaller commitment or an earlier booking, and it is the cheapest of the four.
4. **Change the venue.** Occasionally correct, rarely necessary, and expensive once a date has been announced.

What you must not do is publish the generous version and discover the gap later. Of the five things you can do here, that is the only one that fails in public.

## The policy, in words you can use

Below is a full policy in plain prose, with the blanks for your own dates and amounts. It is deliberately short. A cancellation policy is read in a hurry by someone who is worried about their money, and every sentence they do not need makes the ones they do need harder to find.

> **Refunds and cancellations — [event name], [date]**
>
> **What your fee covers.** Your registration fee of $[amount] covers your place at [event name] on [date], including [what it includes]. It does not cover travel or lodging.
>
> **If you need to cancel.** Tell us in writing at [address] before the deadline for each stage below. The date the message arrives is the date we use.
>
> - **On or before [date 1]:** full refund, or transfer your place to another person at no charge.
> - **Between [date 1] and [date 2]:** refund of your fee less $[amount], which is the part already committed to your place; or transfer your place to another person at no charge.
> - **Between [date 2] and [date 3]:** no refund. You may transfer your place to another person until [date 3].
> - **After [date 3], or if you do not attend:** no refund. Your place was held and paid for.
>
> **Transferring your place.** You may give your place to someone else at any time before [date 3]. Send us their name and email address, and we will move the registration. There is no charge for this.
>
> **If we cancel.** If we cancel the event for any reason, we will refund your full fee to the card you paid with, within [number] business days of the announcement. You do not need to ask.
>
> **If we postpone.** If we move the event to a new date, your fee carries to the new date and your place is held. If you cannot make the new date, tell us before [number] days after we announce it and we will refund your full fee.
>
> **If the event is shortened.** If we have to shorten or materially change the program, we will tell you within [number] days and offer [a partial refund / a credit]. We will not leave you to work out what that means on your own.
>
> **How to reach us.** [Name], [address]. We answer refund requests within one business day.
>
> *This policy was last changed on [date]. If we change it, the change applies to registrations made after that date, and anyone who registered earlier keeps the policy they registered under.*

Two lines in that template are doing more work than they look like they are. The one about the date the message arrives removes the "I sent it on Friday" argument before it starts. The one about answering within a business day is a promise about your own behavior, which is the only kind a refund policy can really make.

### The short version for the registration page

The full policy belongs on its own page. What belongs next to the pay button is six sentences, and they should be the six that decide the purchase:

> Your fee is $[amount]. Cancel in writing before [date 1] for a full refund, or transfer your place to another person at any time before [date 3]. Between [date 1] and [date 2] we refund your fee less $[amount]. After [date 3] there is no refund. If we cancel, you get your full fee back without asking. The full policy is at [link].

A link alone is not a policy, and a policy alone is not read. Put the sentences where the money is decided, and the link underneath them.

### An example of the shape, from our own terms

Convocate's own refund promise is one sentence, and it is worth looking at because of how little it leaves open:

**Full refund, no questions asked, if you ask within 48 hours of the card being charged.**

It names the amount (full), the action (ask), the clock (48 hours), and — this is the part most policies skip — *when the clock starts*: at the charge, not at first use, not at the event. And "no questions asked" removes the negotiation, which is the part people dread. Whatever your deadlines are, that is the shape to copy: amount, action, clock, start of clock, and what you will not ask them to prove.

To be clear about what that example is: it is ours, about the $199 we charge for an event, and the money moves when we move it by hand. It is not a service we run for anyone else. Registration fees and donations at your event are paid into your own Stripe account, not ours, so an attendee's refund is your decision and your payment — we never hold that money and could not return it if we wanted to. The price and what it covers are on [the pricing page](/pricing); the sentence above is here as a model of plain wording, nothing more.

## Cancellation policy wording that starts the argument

Most refund disputes are caused by a phrase, not by a decision. These are the ones worth deleting from the draft.

**"A small handling fee applies."** Small is not a number. If you are keeping part of the money, say how much, at the rung where you keep it. If you are not, delete the sentence.

**"No refunds under any circumstances."** It is honest, and it is also an invitation. A person who reads that and then has a genuine emergency has been told in advance that asking is pointless, so they go to their card issuer instead of to you. A policy with a deadline and a transfer option keeps the conversation in your inbox, where you can be generous if you choose to be.

**"Refunds are at the organizer's discretion."** Discretion without a clock is a promise to argue. Discretion with a named person and a one-day answer is a policy. Keep the discretion if you need it; attach the clock.

**"Tickets are non-transferable."** Often written to stop resale, and it also blocks the person whose daughter's wedding moved. A transfer rule with a deadline gets you most of the protection and none of the resentment.

**"The program is subject to change."** True of every event, and useless as a refund term, because it covers a changed speaker and a cancelled event in the same three words. Say separately what happens if a session changes and what happens if the event does.

And the sixth failure is not a sentence at all: **no policy**, which means the answer is whatever the card network decides under rules you did not write, using evidence you may not have. A dated policy with a deadline on it is evidence. Silence is not.

## Where the policy has to appear

One policy, five places, and they have to agree word for word on the dates and the amounts.

- **The registration page**, next to the price and above the pay button: the six-sentence version, plus a link to the full one.
- **The confirmation email**, in one or two sentences, with the same dates. This is the copy people keep, and it is the copy they produce when they ask.
- **The reminder email before the deadline** — the single most useful message in the whole sequence, because it gives a person who is wavering a fair chance to cancel while they still can. Send it a few days before your last full-refund date, and say the date in the subject line.
- **The ticket or the joining instructions**, in one line, for the person who searches their inbox at the last minute.
- **The answer you send when someone asks**, which should be the policy quoted back with a decision attached, not a fresh negotiation.

Keep one dated master copy in the same place as the budget, with a version line at the bottom. When the policy changes, the version changes, and registrations made before the change keep the policy they were made under. That last sentence is the one that makes a policy defensible a year later, when nobody remembers which version was live in March.

**Name the person who owns it.** One name, with the authority to say yes to a refund inside the policy without asking anyone. If you work from our [committee roles guide](/resources/conference-committee-roles), that is the registration and attendee care seat, and the point of naming it is that the person answering the email is not deciding what the policy is at the same time.

## The first time it is tested

The policy is a document until the first request arrives, and then it is a process. Five habits make it survive.

**Answer within one business day.** Not because the answer is urgent, but because the silence is what people remember. A person who gets a clear no the same day usually stays a supporter. A person who gets a maybe for a week tells the story instead.

**Refund to the same card that paid.** Not to a different card, not in cash, not as a favor to a friend of the attendee. The refund goes back the way the payment came, and that keeps the record clean for both of you.

**Write every request down.** Date received, name, amount, what the policy said, what you decided, who decided it. The log is what makes next year's policy better, and it is what stops two people giving two answers in the same week.

**Notice when the exception is the rule.** One exception is a kindness. Two in a season is a policy that is wrong, and the fix is to change the policy in writing rather than to keep bending it quietly. Bending it quietly is how an event ends up with a published policy, an actual policy and a third one that only two people know about.

**Hold the refund money until the deadline has passed.** The reserve is not spare cash. Once the last full-refund date is behind you, the money that was reserved for refunds becomes budget again — and not one day earlier.

There is one more habit, and it is the cheapest of them: **read the policy out loud before you publish it.** Every sentence that sounds like a lawyer defending a position, rather than a person explaining a decision, will be read as a warning sign. You are not trying to win an argument with an attendee. You are trying to be the organizer whose emails people trust.

## One page to write today

If the event is already open for registration, this is the hour. If it is not, do it before the first fee is taken.

- [ ] Written down what comes back from the venue if you cancel, in dollars, at each stage of the contract.
- [ ] Written down what is already spent and cannot come back: deposit, catering, print, speaker, insurance.
- [ ] Calculated the refund pool: registrations held, minus what is still owed, minus the reserve.
- [ ] Chosen the dates: the last full-refund date, the last partial date, the last transfer date.
- [ ] Checked that the last full-refund date falls before the venue's first non-refundable step.
- [ ] Decided what happens if **you** cancel, and what date the money goes back.
- [ ] Decided what happens if you postpone, shorten or move the event.
- [ ] Set the retained amount in dollars, if you are keeping any.
- [ ] Written the full policy and the six-sentence version, and made sure they agree.
- [ ] Put the six sentences on the registration page, and the policy in the confirmation email.
- [ ] Named the person who answers a refund request, and the one-business-day clock.
- [ ] Dated the master copy, and put it with the budget.
