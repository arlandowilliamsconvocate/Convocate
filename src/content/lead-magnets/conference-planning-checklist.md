# Conference planning checklist
A twelve-month checklist for a 100 to 500 seat conference, in four phases, with an owner named against every line and a one-page summary you can print and hand to somebody else.
Most conference checklists are a list of nouns. Book a venue. Arrange catering. Confirm speakers. Hire AV. They read as though the work does itself once it has been written down, and they leave out the only two things that decide whether a conference is calm or frantic: **when** a thing has to be finished, and **whose** job it is when it is not.

This page fixes that. It covers twelve months out down to one month out, in four phases. Every line names a role, not a person, so the list survives a volunteer leaving. It is written to be printed, filled in and passed around.

**Where this checklist stops, and what picks it up.** This page ends at **one month out**. It does not cover the last month, the week of the event, or the day itself. That is deliberate, because we already publish a guide that owns that ground properly: the [conference day timeline](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-day-timeline) starts where this checklist stops and runs a month out to the last chair, hour by hour. **When you reach the one-month mark, close this file and open that one.** If you use both at once for the same weeks, you will do the work twice and argue with yourself about the order. One checklist, one handoff, one guide for each half of the year.

**What this page does not do.** Five of our other guides own specific pieces of this checklist, and none of them is repeated here. The [conference budget template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-budget-template) owns the cost lines and the arithmetic. The [speaker invitation letter template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-invitation-letter) owns the wording of the ask, and the [speaker contract template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-contract-template) owns the clause language once a number is agreed. The [venue site visit checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/venue-site-visit-checklist) owns the tour — what to measure and count in the room. And if you are stuck on what to offer a speaker, [how much to pay a guest speaker](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-fees-and-honorariums) works through that decision. Where this page touches one of those jobs, it says so and links, rather than writing a shorter, worse version of it.

## How to use this checklist

**Read the whole thing once before you check anything off.** Twenty minutes now saves the argument in month four about who was supposed to do the insurance certificate. You are looking for the lines that make you think "I did not know that was a job" — those go wrong quietly.

**Then work one phase at a time.** Do not print all twelve months and tape them to a wall. Print the phase you are in. A checklist that shows you eleven months of future work at once stops being a plan and becomes a source of dread.

**Fill in the date column, not just a mark.** A mark tells you something happened. A date tells you when, and when is what you need when two things turn out to be scheduled for the same week. If a line was marked done three months ago and the thing it produced cannot be found, it did not happen.

**One owner per line, always.** Not two, not "the committee", not "everyone". Shared ownership is the reason a task sits untouched for a month while four people each assume somebody else has it. If a line genuinely needs two people — a contract that Finance pays and the Event lead signs — name the one who has to make sure it happens, and put the other in the note.

**The owner is a role, and you write the name beside it by hand.** The roles on this page are stable; the people holding them are not. A volunteer steps back, a colleague changes jobs, a committee member has a hard spring. Write the current name and cell phone number next to each role in the margin of your printed copy. When the name changes, you reprint one page instead of rebuilding the plan.

**Give each owner their own lines.** The Finance person should see the six lines they own without reading forty they do not. Print the phase and highlight one role's rows, or copy those rows into a short note. People act on a list of six. They file a list of forty.

**Keep one copy that is the truth.** The most common failure of a shared checklist is that four people each have a slightly different version, and the version that matters is the one in somebody's email. Decide now where the master lives — a shared document, a printed folder at the venue, whatever your team will actually open — and treat every other copy as a printout of it. If two copies disagree, the master wins and the other one is thrown away.

**Expect some lines to move.** That is normal rather than a sign of failure. What matters is that moving a line is a decision somebody makes on purpose and writes down, rather than a date quietly passing. A checklist with three crossed-out dates and a new date beside them is a working document. A checklist with no crossings-out is usually one nobody has read since January.

## The seven roles this checklist uses

Seven is enough for a conference and too many for a small one. If your team is three people, skip to "When the team is three people" — the same lines, distributed differently. What matters is that each of these seven has exactly one person against it, even if that means one person's name appears three times.

| Role | What this role owns, in one line |
|---|---|
| **Event lead** | The date, the room, the budget ceiling, the decisions nobody else can make, and the handoff to the day timeline |
| **Program chair** | What happens on the stage: sessions, speakers, the shape of the day, and whether the program is worth the trip |
| **Finance** | The budget, the deposits, the contracts that cost money, and the one account everybody can see |
| **Venue liaison** | The venue relationship: the site visit, the room, the contract terms, the venue's own staff |
| **Marketing/communications** | Who hears about this, when, in what words, and through which channel |
| **Volunteer coordinator** | How many people are needed, what each one does, and that each one is briefed and fed |
| **Registration** | The attendee list, the fee, the refund rule, the badges, and the desk on the day |

Two rules about these roles, and both of them are learned the hard way.

**The person who spends the money is not the only person who can see the money.** Finance holds the account and the budget, but the Event lead has to be able to look at it without asking permission. A conference where one person is the only one who knows the balance is a conference that finds out about the shortfall in month eleven.

**Whoever can say yes can also say no.** Write down, now, which of these seven can commit money without asking — and up to what amount. If the answer is "we will work it out when it comes up", it will come up in a parking lot with a vendor waiting, and the person who happens to be standing there will decide. That is how a conference ends up with a lighting charge nobody approved.

## Phase one: twelve to nine months out

This phase is not about doing. It is about deciding, and deciding in the right order, because almost every expensive mistake in conference planning is a decision made too early in one place and too late in another. Three months of work here will not look like progress to anybody outside the team. It is the cheapest phase you will get, and it decides the other three.

The single test of this phase: **could you hand somebody a one-page description of this event and have them understand what it is, who it is for, when it is, and roughly what it costs?** If not, keep going. Do not book anything you cannot cancel cheaply until that page exists.

| What has to be done | Owner | Date done |
|---|---|---|
| **Write the purpose in one sentence.** Who this is for and what they leave with. If it takes a paragraph, the event has more than one purpose and one of them will win by accident. | Event lead | |
| **Fix the date, and check it against your audience's calendar before you commit.** School terms, major holidays, the dates of the three or four other events your audience already attends, and your own organization's calendar. Write down what you checked. | Event lead | |
| **Choose a season, not just a date.** A date in a city with a convention in the same week is a date with no hotel rooms and no parking. | Event lead | |
| **Set the attendance you are planning for** as a range: the number you would be pleased with, and the number the room has to hold. Every later decision — the room, the food, the chairs, the badges — is arithmetic on these two numbers. | Event lead | |
| **Agree the budget ceiling before anything is booked.** A total you will not exceed, and who can approve going past it. Build the first version on the [conference budget template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-budget-template), even if the numbers are guesses. | Finance | |
| **Decide how the event is paid for.** Registration fees, sponsorship, an organization subsidy, donations, or a mix. This decides whether registration has to sell and how early it must open. | Finance | |
| **Write down who can commit money, and up to how much,** without asking anyone. Give it to all seven roles in writing. | Event lead | |
| **Confirm the legal and organizational footing.** Who signs contracts on behalf of the organization, whether you are covered by an existing liability policy or need event cover, and whether any permit or license applies to your program, your food, your sound or your signage. | Finance | |
| **Build a shortlist of three venues** that fit your attendance range, your date and your ceiling, and book a site visit at each. Take the [venue site visit checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/venue-site-visit-checklist) with you. | Venue liaison | |
| **Ask each venue for the same thing in writing:** the rate for your date, what the rate includes, the deposit schedule, the cancellation terms, the food and drink minimum if there is one, and how long they will hold the date. | Venue liaison | |
| **Start the speaker list.** Six to eight names, ranked, with why each one fits your audience and what you think they cost. Not one name — one name is not a choice. | Program chair | |
| **Work out what you can pay a speaker** before you approach anyone, using [how much to pay a guest speaker](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-fees-and-honorariums) and your travel assumptions. | Finance | |
| **Write down the shape of the day**, roughly: start and end times, how many sessions, whether there are parallel tracks, how long a session runs, and how many breaks. | Program chair | |
| **Name the seven roles and get a yes from each person.** A role nobody has accepted is a role nobody has. | Event lead | |
| **Decide who your audience hears from first.** Before any public announcement, the people closest to the event — your own organization, your regulars, your partner groups — should hear it from you rather than find it. | Marketing/communications | |

**The decision this phase exists to make:** the date, the ceiling and the shape of the day, in that order. Everything after this is execution.

## Phase two: nine to six months out

This is the phase where the event becomes real and expensive. Contracts get signed, money leaves, and the first public commitments get made. The rule for the whole phase: **nothing is confirmed until it is in writing and the money is accounted for.** A venue that says "we will hold it for you" is holding nothing. A speaker who says "that sounds great" has agreed to nothing.

The test of this phase: **are the venue and the main speaker both under contract, and does the budget have real numbers in it rather than guesses?** If either is no, you are not ready to open registration, however good the website looks.

| What has to be done | Owner | Date done |
|---|---|---|
| **Sign the venue contract and pay the first deposit.** Before signing, check the terms that cost money: what the rate includes, service charges, overtime, load-in and load-out windows, the food and drink minimum, insurance requirements, and the cancellation ladder. | Venue liaison | |
| **Book the room you measured, for the hours you need,** including set-up and load-out. A room booked from 9 a.m. is a room you cannot set up in. | Venue liaison | |
| **Approach the first speaker with a written invitation.** Use the [speaker invitation letter template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-invitation-letter) for the wording, and ask for a reply by a date. | Program chair | |
| **Confirm the speaker in writing** — fee or honorarium, what it covers, travel and lodging, recording and materials, and the date and time they are on stage. The [speaker contract template](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/speaker-contract-template) has the clauses. | Program chair | |
| **Book the speaker's travel and lodging**, or write down clearly that they are booking it themselves and what you will reimburse. Decide this in writing before they buy a ticket. | Program chair | |
| **Replace the budget guesses with real numbers** now that the venue and the speaker are known. Re-check the total against the ceiling. If it does not fit, cut something here rather than in month eleven. | Finance | |
| **Decide the registration fee, and what it includes.** One price or several, what a fee covers, whether there is a group or student rate, and whether the fee is refundable. | Finance | |
| **Write the refund and transfer rule down before anyone can pay.** What happens if they cancel, if you cancel, if they transfer to someone else, and by what date. Publish it where people pay, not in a footnote. | Registration | |
| **Choose how money will be collected**, and test that it actually works: a real transaction, a real receipt, and a refund you have done once. Do this before you tell anyone the price. | Finance | |
| **Set the registration opening date**, working backwards from the date people actually need to decide. | Marketing/communications | |
| **Build the program skeleton:** session titles or topics, how many slots, who fills them, and the deadline for each speaker's title and description. | Program chair | |
| **If you are taking proposals or workshop submissions, open the call and set a hard close date.** A call with no close date stays open forever and fills your inbox in month eleven. | Program chair | |
| **Book the venue's own AV and any outside supplier** you need, and get in writing what is included and what is extra. | Venue liaison | |
| **Arrange insurance** for the date, in whatever wording the venue requires, and apply for any permit or license you identified in phase one. This is arranging it — confirming it is issued and sent to the venue belongs to the day timeline's final month. | Finance | |
| **Recruit the volunteer coordinator's core team**, before you know exactly how many volunteers you need. The person who recruits volunteers should be recruiting from now, not in month two. | Volunteer coordinator | |
| **Decide what you will offer on accessibility** — step-free routes, seating, captioning or a hearing loop, dietary needs, a quiet room — and get the venue's answer on each in writing. Ask now, because some of it is a cost and some of it is a contract term. | Venue liaison | |
| **Put the date in front of your audience** with a save-the-date that has the real date, the real city and one line about who it is for. No price yet if the price is not settled. | Marketing/communications | |

**The decision this phase exists to make:** the venue and the speaker locked, the budget real, and a published price with a published refund rule. That is the last moment at which changing your mind is cheap.

## Phase three: six to three months out

Registration opens in this phase and the event takes a public shape. From here the work is turning things that already exist into things other people can use: a program, a page, a set of contracts, a schedule of messages.

The test of this phase: **could a stranger find the event, pay for it, get a confirmation, and know what to expect when they arrive — without emailing you?** Walk that path yourself as though you were that stranger.

| What has to be done | Owner | Date done |
|---|---|---|
| **Open registration on the date you set**, and buy a ticket yourself on the first day to prove the whole path works. Then refund it. | Registration | |
| **Check the confirmation email** for the three things people need: the date and time, the address, and what happens next. If they have to reply to ask where to park, the email is not finished. | Registration | |
| **Publish the program** with session titles, speaker names and times, at the level of detail people need to decide to come. | Program chair | |
| **Collect every speaker's title, bio, photo and session description** by a deadline you set and enforce. Chase the last two now, not in the final month. | Program chair | |
| **Sign the catering contract** with the numbers you are actually planning for, and get the final-count date in writing. That date is the caterer's, not yours, and it will not move. | Venue liaison | |
| **Contract every other supplier** — AV, printing, signage, photography, transport, childcare if you offer it. The [venue and speaker contract checklist](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) covers what has to be in writing before you sign. | Finance | |
| **Ask every attendee about dietary needs at the point of registration**, and set the date you will pass the list to the caterer. | Registration | |
| **Ask every attendee about accessibility needs at the point of registration**, and name the person who will answer each one. | Registration | |
| **Set the marketing calendar for the rest of the year:** when registration opens publicly, when the program goes out, when the reminder goes, when the last-call goes, and who sends each one. | Marketing/communications | |
| **Ask your partner organizations to share it**, with the words already written for them and a date they will do it. | Marketing/communications | |
| **Write down how many volunteers you need**, by role and by shift, and start filling the shifts by name. | Volunteer coordinator | |
| **Order badges, signs, lanyards and printed programs** with a delivery date at least two weeks before the event — a rule of thumb, not a standard. Everything printed takes longer than it did last year. | Registration | |
| **Review the numbers monthly against the plan**: registered, paid, expected, and the gap. Write the gap down, because it is the number that decides what you cut. | Finance | |
| **Decide what happens when the room fills**, before it does: waitlist, a second room, or a hard close — and who is allowed to make that call. | Event lead | |
| **Confirm the speaker's travel is booked and ticketed**, not just discussed, and send them the practical details: date, time on stage, address, who meets them. | Program chair | |
| **Write the sponsor and partner commitments down** — what they were promised, by when, and who delivers it. Verbal sponsorship is the most common unpaid invoice in events. | Finance | |

**The decision this phase exists to make:** registration open, the program published, suppliers contracted, and the marketing calendar written. From here, you are executing a plan rather than making one.

## Phase four: three to one month out

This is the last phase this page owns. Everything here has to be finished **before** the one-month mark, because the final month belongs to the [conference day timeline](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-day-timeline) and is confirmation rather than work. Arrive at one month out still ordering things, and the final month becomes a rush.

The test of this phase: **is there a piece of paper with every role, every name and every phone number on it, and does every person on it have a copy?** That sheet is the handoff.

| What has to be done | Owner | Date done |
|---|---|---|
| **Re-forecast the budget against actual registrations** and decide what changes: what gets cut, what gets added, and whether the ceiling still holds. | Finance | |
| **Review registration numbers against the room and the caterer's final-count date.** Decide whether to push, hold or close, and write the decision down with a date. | Event lead | |
| **Confirm the speaker's slot in writing one more time** — date, arrival time, session length, what they need on stage, and who meets them at the door. | Program chair | |
| **Proofread the whole program** once, out loud, with someone who has not read it before. Wrong session times and misspelled names on a printed program cannot be fixed on the day. | Program chair | |
| **Collect the last accessibility and dietary requests** and hand each to the person who will act on it, with the date they need it by. | Registration | |
| **Build the owner sheet:** every role, the person's name, their cell phone, and the one thing they are responsible for. Print it for everyone. | Event lead | |
| **Write the final-month communications schedule** — what goes out, on which day, in whose name — and hand it to whoever is running the final month. | Marketing/communications | |
| **Confirm the volunteer roster by name and shift**, and give every volunteer their shift, their arrival time, their position and who to call. | Volunteer coordinator | |
| **Check every printed item is delivered and counted**, not just ordered: badges, signs, programs, lanyards, name tents. Count them against the registration list. | Registration | |
| **Write down how a refund or a transfer is handled** from here on, and who decides, so the answer is the same whoever asks. | Registration | |
| **Do a final pass on the contracts you signed** for anything with a deadline in the last month: payment due dates, final counts, insurance wording, load-in windows. Put those dates on the owner sheet. | Finance | |
| **Hold one meeting with all seven roles**, with the owner sheet in front of everyone, and read the plan aloud. The point is not the reading. The point is that each person hears what the others are doing and finds the gap. | Event lead | |
| **Hand the event over.** Give the day timeline to whoever is running the final month, with the owner sheet and the printed program, and say plainly that the planning checklist is now closed. | Event lead | |

**The decision this phase exists to make:** the plan is finished, written down, and in the hands of the people who will run it. You are done planning. What is left is confirmation, and that is a different job, done by a different page.

## The whole year on one table

The four phases above are the working version. This is the same year compressed into a single view, one line per month, so you can see the order at a glance and check that nothing in the middle is empty. Each line is the thing that has to be **true** by the end of that month, not the thing you start doing in it.

| Months before the event | By the end of this month, this must be true | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| **12** | The purpose is written in one sentence, and the date is chosen and checked against your audience's calendar. | Event lead |
| **11** | The attendance range and the budget ceiling are agreed and written down, and the first budget draft exists. | Finance |
| **10** | Three venues are shortlisted, visited, and compared on the numbers you wrote down in the room. | Venue liaison |
| **9** | The venue is chosen and the deposit terms are understood. The speaker list exists and is ranked. | Venue liaison |
| **8** | The venue contract is signed and the first deposit paid. | Venue liaison |
| **7** | The main speaker is confirmed in writing, with fee, travel and lodging settled. | Program chair |
| **6** | The budget holds real numbers, the fee and the refund rule are decided, and the program skeleton exists. | Finance |
| **5** | Registration is open, and you have bought and refunded a ticket yourself to prove the path works. | Registration |
| **4** | The program is published, and every supplier is under contract with a delivery date. | Program chair |
| **3** | The marketing calendar for the rest of the year is written, and volunteer shifts are filling by name. | Marketing/communications |
| **2** | Printed items are ordered with delivery dates, and dietary and accessibility requests are being collected. | Registration |
| **1** | The owner sheet exists with every role, name and phone number, and every owner has a copy. **This is where this checklist ends.** | Event lead |

If you print one thing from this page, print this table. It fits on a page, it tells you which month you are behind in, and somebody who is not running the event can read it and ask a useful question.

## The five things that actually go wrong

Not the day-of disasters — the day timeline owns those. These are the five failures that damage a conference in the planning year, before anyone has arrived. Each one is common enough that you should assume it is a risk for your event until you have written down the answer.

**1. The venue is booked before the budget ceiling exists.** Booking a venue feels like progress and setting a ceiling feels like pessimism. The result is a room, a deposit and a rate the rest of the event has to fit around, and the only remaining cuts are the ones the audience notices: the food, the program, the speaker. Decide the ceiling first, even if it is a guess with a wide margin. A guess you can revise is better than a commitment you cannot.

**2. The date is chosen before the audience's calendar is checked.** The date gets set by whoever has the room, or by what worked last year, and nobody asks whether the people you want are free. Then you spend the year marketing against a school break, a competing event in the same city, or a week when your own organization is fully occupied. It costs nothing to check this in month twelve and it cannot be fixed in month four. Write down what you checked, so that next year you know what "we checked" meant.

**3. The speaker is confirmed in conversation and nothing is signed.** "They said they are in" is not a confirmation. It is a friendly person being polite on a phone call, and it holds right up until a better offer, a family event or a change of job arrives. What makes it real is a written agreement covering the fee, the travel, the recording, and the date and time on stage — one email to get. If the person will not put it in writing, that is information about the risk, and better to have it in month seven than month one.

**4. Registration opens before the refund rule, the room and the food count are settled.** Money arriving creates obligations. If someone can pay you before you have decided what happens when they cancel, you have promised something you have not defined, and the first person who asks gets an answer you invented on the spot. Settle three things before the page goes live: the refund and transfer rule, the number the room holds, and the date the caterer needs the final count. Then the answer is already written.

**5. Nobody owns the money.** The budget is in one person's spreadsheet, the deposits are in another person's account, the registrations are in a third place, and no single view exists of what has come in and what has gone out. This failure does not announce itself: every individual decision looks reasonable, and the shortfall appears when the invoices arrive after the event. One person holds the money and the budget — that is the rule — and at least one other person can look at both without asking. If you take one line from this page, take that one.

## When the team is three people

Most conferences are not run by seven people. They are run by three people who each hold two or three of the roles above, plus a volunteer coordinator who is also a volunteer. The checklist does not change. The distribution does, and two things have to be decided out loud rather than discovered later.

| Person | Holds | Must not also hold |
|---|---|---|
| **Person A** | Event lead, Venue liaison, Program chair | Finance — not alone, and not the only person who can see the account |
| **Person B** | Finance, Registration | The final say on what gets cut when the budget does not fit |
| **Person C** | Marketing/communications, Volunteer coordinator | Registration, if B is the only other person — somebody has to be able to check the list |

**Decide who breaks a tie.** With three people, two will disagree and the third settles it. Write down who that is before it happens, and that the other two accept it. The alternative is a decision that stalls for three weeks in month five, exactly when registration should be opening.

**Decide what gets dropped first.** Three people cannot do everything a larger team would. Pick in advance the two or three things you will let go if the year gets hard: printed programs, a second track, the social media schedule, the speaker's gift. Deciding this in month twelve means nobody argues about it in month two.

**Give the third person the money question, in writing.** At three people, the person holding Finance also holds Registration, so the registrations and the payments sit in one pair of hands. That is workable. What is not workable is that person being the only one who ever sees the total. Person A looks at the budget once a month, in the room, numbers on screen. Fifteen minutes, twelve times.

**One meeting a month, all three, same agenda.** What is finished, what is late, what has changed, what needs a decision today. Write the four answers down and send them to each other. At three people, the meeting is the project management system, and the written note is the memory.

## When this checklist ends, that guide begins

This checklist stops at one month out on purpose. The last month of a conference is a different kind of work — confirming rather than deciding, checking rather than choosing — and it is already written properly, with the same owner-per-line discipline, in the [conference day timeline](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources/conference-day-timeline).

**What that guide owns, and this one does not:**

- **The final month.** The confirmations that make the last four weeks safe: contracts signed and copies saved, payment dates on a calendar, the deposit receipt filed, the insurance certificate issued and sent in the venue's wording, permits confirmed as approved rather than applied for.
- **The week before and the day before.** The set-up walk, the AV test with real content, the registration desk built in advance, the speaker's arrival and room walk.
- **The day itself, hour by hour.** From the team arriving at 5:30 to the debrief at 6:30, with an owner against every line and blank clock times for you to fill in.
- **The five day-of decisions to make in advance.** The late speaker, the dead microphone, too many people, too few people, and something unsafe.
- **The first 72 hours after.** Invoices against contracts, the deposit chased, payments reconciled, thank-yous sent, numbers written down.

**How to hand over.** At the one-month mark, give that guide, the owner sheet and the printed program to whoever is running the final month, and say the planning checklist is closed. Two lists running at once is how a team does the same job twice and believes it has done it once. One page, one half of the year, then the other.

If you are starting the year rather than finishing it, the rest of the guides are at [getconvocate.com/resources](https://www.getconvocate.com/resources) — the budget template, the speaker invitation letter, the speaker contract template, the venue site visit checklist and the speaker fee guide.
