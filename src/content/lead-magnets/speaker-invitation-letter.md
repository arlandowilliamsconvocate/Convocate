# Speaker invitation letter template

Three letters you can send today, in full, with what to include, what to leave out, and what to settle before you send one.

The speaker invitation letter has one job: make it easy to say yes, and just as easy to say no or to name a number you cannot afford. Most bad invitations fail in one of two directions. Either they are so vague that the person cannot tell what you are asking — how long, where, for whom — or so formal that they read like a form letter and give them nothing to respond to.

Below are three complete letters you can copy and adapt: a keynote, a workshop leader and a breakout session, plus a fill-in-the-blank version. Then the six things every invitation has to say, what to leave out, and the four things to settle before you send anything at all.

Two things belong in the first letter even though they feel uncomfortable: **the money** and **the date you need an answer by**. Leaving out the fee does not read as generous — it reads as though you have not thought about it, and it costs you a week while they work out how to ask. Leaving out the deadline means the answer arrives whenever it arrives, and you cannot plan around it.

This page is the invitation. Once someone says yes, the document changes: what you agreed has to be written down and signed, and that is what the [venue and speaker contract checklist](/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) covers.

## The six things every invitation includes

You cannot write a good invitation until you have decided three things: what exactly you want the person to do, in minutes; how many people, and who they are; and what your number is. These six lines are where those decisions land.

1. **Who you are and what the event is.** Your name, your role, the event name, the dates, the city, the venue. One short paragraph. People check the distance before they read the rest.
2. **Exactly what you are asking for.** The session, the day, the start and end time, the room if you know it, and whether questions are inside that time. A 45-minute keynote with 10 minutes of questions, not "be involved."
3. **Why them, in one specific sentence.** Not "we admire your work" — a talk, a piece of writing, a program they run. One true sentence is the difference between a letter and a mailshot.
4. **The money, or the plain wish to discuss it.** "Our budget for the session is $2,500, and we cover travel and two nights" or "I would like to talk about the fee before you answer, because I would rather hear your number than guess." Both are honest. Silence is not. If you have not set that budget line, the [conference budget template](/resources/conference-budget-template) has a speaker fee line and a way to size it.
5. **What you need from them, and by when.** A reply by a date. Say that once they agree you will ask for a session description, a short bio, a headshot and any equipment they need — then ask for those after the yes.
6. **One next step.** A 20-minute call, or "reply yes and I will send a short written agreement." One, not three.

## The keynote invitation letter

The one to send when you are asking a named person to carry the main session. It is deliberately short — about 300 words without the bracketed notes — because a keynote invitation that arrives long reads like work.

> **Subject:** Invitation to speak at [Conference name], [Month DD–DD, Year]
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> My name is [your name], and I am [the program chair / on the planning committee] for [Conference name], which takes place [dates] at [venue] in [city].
>
> On behalf of the planning committee, I am writing to invite you to speak. We are expecting about [number] people — [one honest clause: who they are and where they come from]. Your session would be the [opening keynote / closing session] on [day], from [start time] to [end time], including time for questions.
>
> I am asking you because [one specific, true sentence about their work and why it fits what your attendees are dealing with].
>
> We would like to settle the fee early rather than at the end, so nothing is awkward later. Our budget for the session is [amount], and we cover [travel and how many nights of lodging]. If that is not what you would normally expect, please tell me — I would rather hear it now than have you turn down something you would otherwise have done.
>
> If it is useful, I would like [20 minutes on a call next week] to talk through the shape of the session and answer anything about the event. If you would rather decide from this letter, that is fine too.
>
> If you can join us, I will send a short written agreement covering the fee, travel, recording and what happens if either side has to cancel, so we both have the same thing on paper.
>
> I would be grateful for a reply by [date] — we are holding the slot until then.
>
> With thanks for considering it,
>
> [Name]
> [Title, organization]
> [Phone and email]

Three lines in that letter are doing quiet work. **"We are holding the slot until then"** gives the deadline a reason. **"I would rather hear it now"** gives them permission to name a number higher than yours. **"So we both have the same thing on paper"** tells them a contract is coming, which reassures a professional and filters out anyone who does not want one.

## The workshop leader letter

A different ask: less performance, more design, and usually a closer relationship. It is shorter and more collaborative, and it asks what they need in the room — which is where workshops fail.

> **Subject:** Would you lead a workshop at [Conference name] on [date]?
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> I am [name], [role] for [Conference name], on [dates] at [venue] in [city]. We expect about [number] attendees — [one line on who they are].
>
> I would like to ask you to lead a [90-minute] workshop on [topic] on [day] at [time], for about [25–40] people, because [the specific experience that makes them right for this room].
>
> The session is meant to be practical: we would like people to leave with [the specific thing — a draft plan, a filled-in checklist, a decision]. If you want handouts, laptops or a particular room setup, tell me and we will arrange it.
>
> We can offer [amount] for the session, and we cover [travel and lodging]. If the number is not right, say so — we would rather adjust the scope than lose you.
>
> Once you say yes I will send a short written agreement covering the fee, what you need in the room, recording, and cancellation on both sides. Could you let me know by [date]?
>
> Thank you for considering it,
>
> [Name]
> [Title, organization]
> [Phone and email]

The line about what people should leave with is the most useful sentence here. It tells the leader whether they already have a workshop that fits, and it saves you a planning meeting.

## The breakout session letter

Breakouts are a smaller ask, usually to someone closer to home: a practitioner, a local expert, someone from your own membership. Match the tone. Writing to it as though it were a headline booking makes people suspicious.

> **Subject:** A breakout session at [Conference name], [date]
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> I am [name], and I am putting the program together for [Conference name] on [date] at [venue] in [city]. We expect about [number] attendees.
>
> We are running [four] breakout sessions in parallel on the [afternoon of day one], each about [45] minutes for [20–30] people. I would like to ask you to take one on [topic], based on [the specific thing they do — the way they run intake, the system they built, the program they have run for years].
>
> This is a small room rather than the main stage: a short talk and then questions from people doing the same work you are. We can offer [amount], and [we cover parking and lunch / we do not expect you to travel, since you are local].
>
> If you are interested, I will send a short note confirming the time, the room, the fee and the equipment that is already there. For the program I would need a two-line description of the session and a short bio by [date]. Would you let me know by [date]?
>
> Thank you,
>
> [Name]
> [Title, organization]
> [Phone and email]

## The fill-in-the-blank version

If you would rather write it yourself and just want to be sure nothing is missing, this is the whole letter as a skeleton. Every bracket is a decision you have to make before you send.

> Dear [Name],
>
> I am [your name], [your role] for [event name], which takes place [dates] at [venue] in [city]. We expect about [number] people, mostly [who they are].
>
> I am writing to invite you to [speak / lead a workshop / take a breakout session] on [topic], on [day] from [start] to [end], [including / not including] questions.
>
> I am asking you because [one true, specific sentence].
>
> [We can offer [amount] for the session, and we cover [travel and lodging].] OR [I would like to talk about the fee before you answer.]
>
> If you say yes, I will send a short written agreement covering the fee, travel, recording and cancellation, and I will ask you for [a session description, a short bio, a headshot] and anything you need in the room.
>
> Could you let me know by [date]?
>
> [Name], [title], [organization], [phone], [email]

## What to include and what to leave out

| Include | Leave out |
|---|---|
| The date, the start time, the length in minutes | The whole program, or how many other sessions there are |
| The venue and the city, named | A request for slides — ask once they know the room |
| How many people, and who they are | The fee you are paying somebody else |
| One specific reason you are asking them | The names of others you have asked for the same slot |
| The fee, or the plain wish to discuss it | A paragraph about your organization's history |
| A reply-by date with a reason | "At your earliest convenience" — give them a day |
| One next step | Three asks in one paragraph: speak, run a workshop, sit on a panel |
| The name, phone and email of one person | A CC to the whole planning committee |
| The line that a short written agreement will follow | Flattery that is not specific to them |

Two of those are worth spelling out, because they are the ones people get wrong.

**Include the line about the agreement.** It is not intimidating; it is the opposite. A professional wants to know a document is coming, because the document is what protects them.

**Leave out the other names.** Telling someone who else you have asked, before they answer, reads as pressure, and it can create a real problem if the two people have history. You can honestly say you are speaking to a small number of people about the slot. You do not have to say who.

## What to agree before you send the letter

You do not have to settle all of this in the invitation. You do have to know your own answer before you write, because the reply will ask — and answering "let me check" three times costs you the booking.

**The fee.** Decide the number, or the ceiling, before you write. Say what it covers: one session or two, whether questions are included, whether materials are in it, and whether recording is extra. A fee that covers "the session" and turns out to mean one 45-minute talk is a fee you will renegotiate in a hallway. [What to pay a guest speaker](/resources/speaker-fees-and-honorariums) covers fee versus honorarium and how to arrive at a number.

**Travel.** Decide who books, and cap what you will reimburse. "We will cover your travel" is not a number, and it is the line that breaks budgets. Give a class of travel or a maximum, the nights of lodging you will pay for and which nights, and whether parking and meals come out of it. Whoever books owns the change fees.

**Recording.** Decide before you ask, because some speakers charge more for it and all of them will ask. Say whether you want video, audio or both; whether you will post it, sell it or use clips; whether you may photograph them and use the images later; and whether they may record and post their own session.

**Cancellation.** Decide what you will pay if you cancel, and what you expect back if they do. The version most organizers land on: the deposit is returned if the speaker cancels without a documented reason, the balance is due after the event, and a cancellation by you close to the date means the full fee. Write it down. It is the clause nobody negotiates later and everybody argues about afterwards.

Those four, written down and signed, are the speaker agreement. [What to confirm in writing before you sign](/resources/vendor-contract-checklist) takes them one at a time, with the three clauses people most often regret. The document itself is on the [speaker contract template](/resources/speaker-contract-template) page — eighteen clauses, every blank named, and a plain-English note on what each one protects.

## If they say yes

Five things, in this order, in the first week. It takes an hour and it prevents most of the confusion that shows up in the last month.

1. **Send the short written agreement** while the conversation is warm: fee, travel, recording, cancellation, one page.
2. **Put the fee on the budget as its own line**, with travel beside it rather than inside it, so you can see what the session actually costs.
3. **Ask for what you need for the program** — session title and description, short bio, headshot, equipment and room setup — with a date.
4. **Pass the technical needs to whoever runs your AV**, in writing, at the same time you book the room.
5. **Confirm the day-of details a week out**: arrival time, who meets them, where they park, what time they are on.

If the event has a public page, get the session listed on it. A speaker who can see themselves on the program tells other people.

## If they say no, or do not reply

Send one short nudge, seven to ten days after the reply-by date, and make it easy to answer with one word: "I know this may have arrived at a busy moment — we are holding the slot until [date], and a simple no is a perfectly good reply." Then move on.

Approach more than one person for the same slot if the slot matters, and say so in the letter: "we are speaking to a small number of people about this session." What you must not do is confirm two people for one slot, or let someone believe they are the only ask while you are writing to three.
